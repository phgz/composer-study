## Techniques

1. **One short note value from end to end.** In the two quartet scherzos, 78% and 84% of all the notes are eighths; in the Octet's the running notes are sixteenths.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* write a quick movement almost entirely in one short note value, running without a break.
   *Where:* String Quartets op. 44 nos. 2 and 3, the scherzos (eighths); the Octet, third movement (sixteenths). **[score]**
2. **Pianissimo and staccato throughout** (the Octet: *Allegro leggierissimo*, with the composer's own warning to keep it so). In the quartets soft is the ground and loud is a jab: against 80 and 118 markings of *p* and *pp* stand 51 and 88 sudden accents (*sf*), about one every four or five bars.
   *Works on:* loudness, colour.
   *Why:* to keep the music light, detached and airy, the fairy world of his later *Midsummer Night's Dream* overture. [Sussex] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* keep the whole movement very soft and short; break the softness with a single sudden accent every four or five bars, never with a passage that is loud.
   *Where:* the Octet's scherzo (the marking and the warning) [Sussex]; the scherzos of op. 44 nos. 2 and 3 (the counts). **[score: op. 44 nos. 2 and 3]**
3. **The running line changes hands about every three bars.** 75 times in 199 bars, 102 times in 290.
   *Works on:* texture.
   *How:* pass the running figure from one instrument to another every two to four bars, so that it never stays with one player.
   *Where:* the scherzos of op. 44 nos. 2 and 3. **[score: op. 44 nos. 2 and 3]**
4. **Repeated notes inside the running.** One quick step in seven to ten is the same note again.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *How:* in the running line, let about one note in eight repeat the note before it.
   *Where:* the four scherzos read. **[score]**
5. **It ends by vanishing.** The Octet's scherzo closes on an arpeggio rising through the chord of its keynote to the top of the violin, pianissimo and fading, "in a puff of air"; the piano scherzo ends on a rising broken chord too. The quartet scherzos end *p*.
   *Works on:* form, loudness.
   *Why:* the sound disperses instead of closing with force, a weightless end. [Sussex] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* end with a broken chord of the keynote rising to the top of the highest instrument, very soft and fading, with no final chord struck.
   *Where:* the Octet's scherzo, the last bars; the Scherzo op. 16 no. 2, the last bars. **[score; Sussex]**
6. **Once, a single violin comes forward** with trills and smoother playing, in the middle (Octet).
   *Works on:* texture, colour.
   *Why:* a smoother, harder passage set against the staccato before it returns, and a place for the dedicatee, Eduard Rietz, the first violinist. [Sussex] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* once in the middle, let one instrument step out of the running texture with a smoother line and trills, then return.
   *Where:* the Octet's scherzo, the middle. [Sussex]
7. **Where it came from.** The Octet's scherzo was prompted, his sister said, by lines of Goethe about clouds and mist passing and the sky brightening; it leads to the *Midsummer Night's Dream* overture, written just after.
   *Works on:* background.
   *How:* not a technique: the picture behind it, mist and cloud passing, which may guide its lightness.
   *Where:* the Octet's scherzo; the *Midsummer Night's Dream* overture. [Sussex]
8. **Metres:** two (Octet), three (op. 44 no. 2), six-eight (op. 44 no. 3), four (op. 16 no. 2). Long in bars, short in time: 244 to 311 bars.
   *Works on:* rhythm, form.
   *How:* the metre may be any; what makes the scherzo is the speed: many bars, short in time.
   *Where:* the four scherzos read. **[score]**

### The organ fugues, op. 37

The Three Preludes and Fugues op. 37 are dedicated to Thomas Attwood, Mozart's pupil, and form a pendant to the six preludes and fugues for piano, op. 35 [BYU organ wiki, quoting Grove]. In the catalogue they are three movements in four voices, the lowest on the pedals: C minor, 65 bars of six quarters; G major, 63 bars of eight; D minor, 95 bars of four.

9. **The answer comes a fourth above.** In the D minor fugue the subject enters on D (bar 1, in a middle voice), on G (bar 5, the top), on D (bar 9, a low voice) and on G (bar 13, the pedals): four voices, one every four bars, the answer on the fourth above the keynote rather than on the fifth.
   *Works on:* form, harmony.
   *How:* bring the four voices in one at a time, four bars apart, alternating the subject on the keynote with the subject a fourth higher; the last of the four in the bass.
   *Where:* op. 37 no. 3, D minor, bars 1, 5, 9 and 13. **[score: op. 37 no. 3, fugue]**
10. **The entries come closer.** Later in the D minor fugue, entries follow one another after three or two beats (bars 36 to 39, 46 and 47, 80). In the C minor fugue, from bar 24 to bar 38, an entry begins in nearly every bar, each in another voice; at bars 32 to 35 they enter on D, G, C and F, each a fourth above the last.
    *Works on:* form, texture.
    *How:* after the opening entries, let the subject come back more and more often: first a few bars apart, then a bar apart, then a few beats apart, in a different voice each time; a chain of entries can climb by fourths, one a bar.
    *Where:* op. 37 no. 3, D minor, the bars named; op. 37 no. 1, C minor, bars 24 to 38. **[score: op. 37 nos. 1 and 3, fugues]**
11. **The bass keeps the subject for the end.** After its first entry (bar 13), the D minor fugue's pedal part carries the subject at bar 39, then three times in the last third (bars 57, 60 and 70), before a last pair of entries two beats apart (bar 80). The G major fugue begins with the subject in the pedals.
    *Works on:* form, texture.
    *How:* after the bass's first entry, keep the subject out of it for a long stretch; give it to the bass again near the end, more than once, under the others.
    *Where:* op. 37 no. 3, the bars named; op. 37 no. 2, bar 1. **[score: op. 37 nos. 2 and 3, fugues]**
12. **The D minor subject:** three long notes (up a step, down a fifth), a rest, a leap of more than an octave, and a fall by step in quarters with a turn near its end, about four bars in all.
    *Works on:* melody.
    *How:* begin a subject with a few long notes that leap, stop, leap up high and come down by step in even notes, turning once on the way.
    *Where:* op. 37 no. 3, bars 1 to 4. **[score: op. 37 no. 3, fugue]**
13. **The C minor subject: one low note struck again between the steps of a rising line** (G, C, G, D, G, E flat), short-long, short-long, then falling by a sequence.
    *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
    *How:* alternate a repeated low note with the notes of a line rising by step, in short-long pairs; then let the line fall in the same pattern, a step lower each time.
    *Where:* op. 37 no. 1, bars 1 to 3. **[score: op. 37 no. 1, fugue]**
14. **Where it comes from.** His organ music is called the first major German contribution to solo organ music since Bach; he was instrumental in reviving the playing of Bach's works, and conducted the first performance of the *St Matthew Passion* since Bach's death. In his fugues above all, the page says, his music sounds almost wholly Baroque. Five manners are named in his organ works: the English Baroque voluntary, the chorale, the fugue or fugato, the Baroque toccata, and melodic ornament.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: where the fugues stand.
    *Where:* [BYU organ wiki, partly quoting Grove and Seaton]

## Walks through whole works

**Op. 37 no. 3, the fugue in D minor** (95 bars of four quarters), by its entries. The exposition: D, G, D, G, at bars 1, 5, 9 and 13, the pedals last. Entries in other keys: A (bar 19), A and D (25), C (33). From bar 36 the entries come two or three beats apart (36 to 39, 46 and 47). The pedals take the subject at bar 39, then at 57, 60 and 70. A last pair two beats apart at bar 80; the fugue ends at bar 95.

From piece 64's search of its own lines in the 75 works that could be read: his quick even notes move by step so often that small figures are common property. A neighbour note and six steps down, in even notes, is in thirteen of the works; a turn on the leading note and back, in seven; a turn followed by eight steps up the scale, in one (the finale of the quartet op. 12).

## Not checked

- The Octet's dynamics and staccato marks: the file read holds none, so "pianissimo and staccato throughout" rests on the programme note.
- **The Octet was read from the editor's copy for playing, not the printed score** (score-reader, version 24 on: `midi_copy`, a `\score` that only writes MIDI, with `\articulate`). That copy plays the ornaments out and shortens the notes, and it may double voices. Techniques 1 (the Octet's running sixteenths) and 5 (the closing arpeggio) come from it; the quartets' counts do not. Not compared with the printed score.
- **The organ fugues were read from the editor's copy for playing** too (`midi_copy`). Where the entries begin was found by the subject's first three or four notes, in every voice; an entry whose first notes the composer changed (a tonal answer) is not found that way, so the lists may miss entries, and two in the same staff at once may be one voice and a chord. The registrations and the speeds are not in the reading.
- Whether the answers at the fourth are his habit or this fugue's: only op. 37 was read.
- Anything about the *Midsummer Night's Dream* music itself.
- The Songs Without Words, the symphonies, the oratorios, the organ sonatas op. 65: not covered.
