# Antonín Dvořák (1841-1904)

Begun by Claude on 22 November 2026; extended on 24 November 2026 for composer-blend's piece 70, when he was drawn again, and for piece 70b; on 25 November 2026 for piece 75, when he was chosen again: the furiant's cross-rhythm measured in the scherzo of the ninth symphony (16); and on 27 November 2026 for piece 82b, when he was drawn again: the String Quartet no. 12, op. 96, "American" (17 to 20). It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** Three kinds of piece: the dumka and the furiant, which he often joins; and the polka, as the second movement of the String Quartet no. 9 in D minor, op. 34 (1877), shows it. **Scores:** yes. score-reader's catalogue (version 20) holds 61 of his works: the seventh and ninth symphonies, six string quartets, the *Silhouettes* and other piano pieces. The Dumka of the String Quartet in E flat op. 51 was read whole by measurement, and the scherzo of the seventh symphony for its metre; the polka of op. 34 was read from Philip's ready-read copy (catalogue version 25), its bass whole and its first twenty bars note by note; each technique marked **[score]** says where. The Slavonic Dances and the *Dumky* Trio are not in the catalogue.

## Techniques

1. **A dumka goes suddenly from melancholy to high spirits and back.** The word is a small *duma*, a brooding ballad. Usually slow and dreamy in two; the lively part breaks in.
   *Works on:* form.
   *How:* a slow, brooding section in two, broken into without warning by a lively one, and back again, more than once.
   *Where:* the dumkas listed in technique 11. [Wikipedia, Dumka]
2. **One keynote, and the mode flips.** The quartet's Dumka: slow in G minor, quick in G major, slow in G minor, the slow tune once in G major, then a Presto in G minor. The keynote never moves.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *Why:* the keynote stays, so the change of mood is made by the mode alone. **[score: op. 51, second movement]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* keep one keynote for the whole piece and change only the mode: minor for the slow part, major for the quick, the slow tune once in the major, the last quick part in the minor.
   *Where:* String Quartet op. 51, second movement (Dumka). **[score: op. 51, second movement]**
3. **Slow in two, quick in three-eight.** *Andante con moto*, 2/4; *Vivace*, 3/8; and *Presto*, 3/8, to close.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* the slow part in 2/4, the quick parts in 3/8, the last the fastest.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement. **[score: op. 51]**
4. **The quick part is longer than the slow part it interrupts.** 87 bars slow, 123 quick; then 20 and 22 slow, 50 quick. At the file's speeds the slow parts still last longer.
   *Works on:* form.
   *How:* give the interrupting quick part more bars than the slow part, but let the slow part last longer in time.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement: bars 1 to 87, 88 to 210, 211 to 252, 253 to 302. **[score: op. 51]**
5. **A long note thrown across the bar line.** In the *Vivace* the commonest bar of the first violin is two eighths and a quarter that begins on the last eighth and runs into the next bar (31 such quarters in 123 bars). Next commonest: two sixteenths and an eighth; three eighths.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *Why:* the long note begins on the bar's last eighth and runs over the bar line, so the weight falls where the metre has none. **[score: op. 51]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* in 3/8, a bar of two eighths and then a quarter that starts on the third eighth and is held over into the next bar; mix it with bars of two sixteenths and an eighth, and of three eighths.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement, the *Vivace*, first violin. **[score: op. 51]**
6. **The furiant's count: 2+2+2+3+3.** A fast Bohemian dance in three, whose accents make three groups of two out of the first two bars, then two plain bars.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *Why:* the irregular accents are what make its rhythm wild; the dancer's jumps and stamps follow them. [Wikipedia, Furiant] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* in 3/8, a unit of four bars: accent the first, third and fifth eighths of the first pair of bars, then the first eighth of each of the next two.
   *Where:* the furiants listed in technique 10. [Wikipedia, Furiant]
7. **How the dance is laid out** (the *Vivace*'s first sixteen bars): the two violins together in one rhythm, the viola in sixteenths, the cello in steady eighths.
   *Works on:* texture.
   *How:* in a quick dance for four, the two highest in the same rhythm, one inner part running in quicker notes, the bass in steady notes.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement, the first sixteen bars of the *Vivace* (from bar 88). **[score: op. 51]**
8. **The speed gives and takes.** *A tempo* again and again in the slow part; *poco meno mosso* and *poco più mosso* inside the quick one; a slowing over six bars before the slow part returns.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* let the speed ease and return often in the slow part, slow a little and quicken a little inside the quick one, and slow down over several bars before the slow part comes back.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement; the slowing comes before the slow part returns at bar 211. **[score: op. 51]**
9. **The last quick part is the fastest and in the minor.**
   *Works on:* harmony, rhythm, form.
   *How:* make the final quick part faster than the first one and put it in the minor.
   *Where:* op. 51, second movement, the *Presto* (bars 253 to 302). **[score: op. 51]**
10. **Where his furiants are:** Slavonic Dances op. 46 nos. 1 and 8; the scherzo of the sixth symphony (bars of 3/4 and 3/2 by turns); the third movements of the seventh and ninth symphonies; the last movement of the violin concerto, whose middle is a dumka. The seventh's is written in 6/4, where two threes and three twos fill the same bar.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* a list of places to read; in 6/4, two threes and three twos can share one bar.
    *Where:* as listed; the seventh symphony's third movement read in score. [Wikipedia, Furiant; Wikipedia, Dumka] **[score: Symphony no. 7, third movement]**
11. **Where his dumkas are:** op. 35 and op. 12 for piano, the sextet op. 48, the quartet op. 51, the piano quintet op. 81, three of the Slavonic Dances, and the *Dumky* Trio op. 90, six movements that are each one.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* a list of places to read.
    *Where:* as listed. [Wikipedia, Dumka]

### The polka of the String Quartet no. 9, op. 34

The polka is a dance in 2/4 from nineteenth-century Bohemia; its name refers to its half-step, the dancers' half jump [Wikipedia, Polka]. Op. 34 was finished on 18 December 1877 and is dedicated to Brahms [Wikipedia, String Quartet No. 9].

12. **The bass stays on one note, like a drone.** For the first sixteen bars the cello plays only B flat: in octaves, low and high by turns in eighths, then as repeated notes, while the violins play the tune. It comes back: in bars 57 to 72 the cello spends three quarters of its time on one note (F, then B flat). Between, bars 17 to 56, it moves through six to nine notes in every eight bars.
    *Works on:* harmony, texture, form.
    *How:* hold the bass on one note for the dance's first strain, as octaves or repeated notes; let it move in the middle; bring the drone back with the tune.
    *Where:* op. 34, second movement, bars 1 to 16 and 57 to 72. **[score: op. 34, second movement]**

13. **The tune in two violins a third apart.** The first and second violins begin in the same rhythm, a third apart (F and G over D and E flat).
    *Works on:* texture, melody.
    *How:* double the dance tune a third below in the same rhythm, as technique 7 does in the dumka's quick part.
    *Where:* op. 34, second movement, bars 1 to 2. **[score: op. 34, second movement]**

14. **A hop on one repeated note.** Under the tune, the second violin plays a dotted eighth, a sixteenth and an eighth all on one note (D), bar after bar, five bars running, over the drone.
    *Works on:* rhythm.
    *How:* give an inner part one note in a dotted rhythm, repeated bar after bar, as the dancers' hop.
    *Where:* op. 34, second movement, bars 14 to 18. **[score: op. 34, second movement]**

### *Echo of Songs*, B. 152 (the *Cypresses* for string quartet)

Dvořák chose twelve of the eighteen love songs of his *Cypresses* (1865) and arranged them for string quartet in 1887, keeping their melodic line, rhythm and harmony, with some exchange of lines between the instruments [Wikipedia, Cypresses (quartet version)].

15. **The song without its singer, passed between the instruments over a held bass.** In the first of them the cello repeats A flat seventeen times, a note a beat (bars 2 to 6). Measured by spans of eight bars, the longest notes (the song's) lie in the viola for the second to fourth spans, in the first violin for the sixth to eighth, and in the viola again at the end.
    *Works on:* texture, melody, colour.
    *How:* give a song's line to the viola, then to the first violin, then back, over a bass that repeats one note.
    *Where:* *Echo of Songs*, no. 1. **[score: Echo of Songs, no. 1]**

### The scherzo of the ninth symphony, read from the score (for piece 75)

The third movement of the Symphony no. 9 (1893), *Molto vivace*, in 3/4, read from Mutopia's edition in score-reader's catalogue (version 25): 301 bars, fourteen parts. The reading names its parts only by number, so they are given here by register. Measured: a part is "in twos" across a pair of bars when it strikes three notes of two beats or more at beats 1, 3 and 5 of the six, and nothing else of a beat or more; "in threes" when it holds a note of most of a bar at beats 1 and 4.

16. **The twos in some parts, the threes kept in others, and one part's twos a bar late.** The cross-rhythm is never the whole orchestra. In 14 pairs of bars (a pair counted from any bar) at least one part moves in twos. In bars 50 to 55 five parts are in twos (four upper and middle parts and a low one; t1 to t4 and t11 of the reading) while one high part (t6) keeps a note a bar. In bars 123 to 129 up to seven parts are in twos against two in threes, and one middle part's twos begin a bar after the others', so that its pairs of bars overlap theirs.
    *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
    *Why:* measured: both counts sound at once, the twos carried by some parts over threes held by others, and in bars 123 to 129 the twos themselves are staggered by a bar between groups. **[score: Symphony no. 9, third movement]** *(from the score)*
    *How:* in 3/4, give a group of parts three notes of two beats across two bars while another group holds a note a bar; then start one part's twos a bar later than the rest.
    *Where:* Symphony no. 9, third movement, bars 42 to 55 and 123 to 129 (and 247 and 248). **[score: Symphony no. 9, third movement]**

### The "American" quartet, op. 96 (read for piece 82b)

Read on 27 November 2026: the first two movements by measurement from score-reader's catalogue (version 25, OpenScore's edition), the first and second violins, viola and cello as parts 1 to 4, and the sources below. The second movement, *Lento*, is in 6/8 with one flat, 97 bars; the first, *Allegro ma non troppo*, 4/4, 178 bars.

17. **The viola opens with a pentatonic tune over a shimmer.** Bar 1: the violins alternate the notes of an F major chord (F and A, A and C) as a trembling floor; from bar 3 the viola plays the tune, every one of its 27 notes in bars 3 to 6 on F, G, A, C or D, the five-note scale without the fourth and seventh. The tune later goes to the first violin.
    *Works on:* melody, colour.
    *Why:* "Dvořák, a viola player himself, gives the viola the opening pentatonic theme" [Sussex] *(an analysis)*; he gives "the rich voice of his own instrument, the viola, a pre-eminent role" [West Cork] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* let the inner instrument state the tune first, alone in its register, using only the five notes of the major pentatonic scale, over the two violins holding the chord in quick alternating notes; give the tune to the first violin later.
    *Where:* op. 96, first movement, bars 1 to 6. **[score: op. 96, first movement]** [Sussex; antonin-dvorak.cz; Britannica]
18. **The slow tune unspools over one bar repeated.** In the *Lento* the viola plays the same six-note figure twice a bar (F, A, A, D, D, A, in sixteenths) bar after bar: its rhythm is the bar before's in 78 of 96 bars, its notes too in 28; the second violin repeats D and F (D, F, F, F twice a bar), the cello plucks D. Over that the first violin enters at bar 3 with the tune, 100 of its 125 notes in bars 1 to 20 on D, F, G, A or C (D minor's pentatonic). The tune passes to the cello and back (the cello holds the longest notes of the bar in 18 bars, the first violin in 57).
    *Works on:* melody, texture, rhythm.
    *Why:* "The first violin and cello's elongated melodic lines unspool over a rippling texture in the inner voices," joining in a duet [Princeton University Concerts' note] *(a listener)*; the opening theme "has a rhythmically ostinato" accompaniment [Sussex] *(an analysis)*. Measured: the floor under the tune is one bar, kept. **[score: op. 96, second movement]** *(from the score)*
    *How:* write one bar of accompaniment for the inner voices (a six-note broken chord in the viola, a two-note rocking figure in the second violin, a plucked bass note) and keep it bar after bar while a long tune, mostly on the minor pentatonic, unfolds over it in the first violin; hand the tune to the cello for a phrase, then back.
    *Where:* op. 96, second movement, bars 1 to 20, and the cello's bars (18, 34, 42, 88, 95, 97 are the bars where its notes are longest). **[score: op. 96, second movement]** [Princeton University Concerts; Sussex]
19. **"Something for once that was very melodious and straightforward"**: his defence of the quartet's simplicity (Wikipedia's translation). Written in Spillville, Iowa, among Czech settlers, in June 1893: sketched in three days and completed in thirteen more, the score finished with "Thank God! I am content." What makes it "American" is most strongly "the overall simplicity of the quartet's structure and its reliance on the pentatonic scales" [Apple Music Classical].
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* (a background entry, no recipe).
    *Where:* op. 96. [Wikipedia, String Quartet No. 12 (Dvořák); antonin-dvorak.cz; Apple Music Classical]
20. **His words of 1893:** "the new American school of music must strike its roots deeply into its own soil", and "I am now satisfied that inspiration for truly national music might be derived from the Negro melodies or Indian chants" (quoted in Princeton University Concerts' note, beside William Grant Still's answer of 1950: see Still's page, 17).
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* (a background entry, no recipe).
    *Where:* (his writing, not a work). [Princeton University Concerts]

## What he does not do

*In this movement.*

- Change keynote between the slow and the quick.
- Let the quick part end the first time: it slows into the return.

## Too well known to borrow

- **The *Lento* of the "American" quartet**, "the celebrated slow movement" [Princeton University Concerts]: its tune is not taken, only its way of laying a tune over a repeated bar (18).
- **The Largo's theme from the ninth symphony, "From the New World"**, on the cor anglais: called its famous main theme; made into the song "Goin' Home" (1922), often taken for a folk song, and familiar in Britain from a television advert of 1973. The symphony is among the most popular of all. [Wikipedia, Symphony 9]
