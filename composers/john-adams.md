# John Adams (born 1947)

## Techniques

### Repeating cells (*Shaker Loops*, *Phrygian Gates*)

1. **Loops of strings shaking between notes.** *Shaker Loops*, first for a string septet and then for string orchestra, keeps the idea of repeating loops of oscillations on the strings; its title is for the "shaking" of the strings as they oscillate between notes, and for the Shakers dancing to repetitive, energetic music. It was first called *Wavemaker* and meant to imitate the ripples of water.
   *Works on:* texture, rhythm, colour.
   *How:* give each string part a short cell that oscillates between two notes and repeats; let the parts' cells overlap like ripples.
   *Where:* *Shaker Loops*. [Wikipedia, Shaker Loops]
2. **Repeating cells that build large shapes.** He used "the fabric of continually repeating cells to forge large architectonic shapes".
   *Works on:* form, texture.
   *Why:* the cells' repetition is the material from which the large shapes are built. [Wikipedia, John Adams] *(his words)*
   *How:* make the long form out of the cells themselves, by how many play and for how long, not out of new themes.
   *Where:* *Shaker Loops*. [Wikipedia, John Adams]
3. **Gates: a square wave between two modes.** *Phrygian Gates* (25 to 30 minutes) is built of repeating cells and goes round the circle of fifths through half the keys: A Lydian, A Phrygian, E Lydian, E Phrygian, and so on, each key bright (Lydian) then dark (Phrygian); the time spent in the Lydian gets shorter as the piece goes on. The gates are named after the electronic gates of synthesizers, a term for switching quickly between states.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *Why:* he describes it as "in the form of a modulating square wave", one state Lydian and the other Phrygian. [Wikipedia, Phrygian Gates] *(his words)*
   *How:* hold a keynote and switch its scale between the Lydian (raised fourth) and the Phrygian (lowered second, third, sixth, seventh), back and forth; move the keynote round the circle of fifths; make the bright state shorter each time.
   *Where:* *Phrygian Gates*. [Wikipedia, Phrygian Gates]
4. **A steady pulse,** as the other minimalists of his time, to define and control the music.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* keep one even pulse under everything.
   *Where:* his music of these years. [Wikipedia, John Adams]
5. **Minimalism with the climaxes of late Romanticism:** developed from Reich and Glass, but more ready to build the large orchestral textures and climaxes of Wagner, Mahler and Sibelius; repeating patterns without strict adherence to the movement.
   *Works on:* form, loudness, background.
   *How:* repeat and pulse as the minimalists do, but let the music swell to a climax.
   *Where:* his music in general. [Wikipedia, John Adams]
6. **Tonality as a force of nature:** his "diatonic conversion", from working with synthesizers; *Harmonielehre* called "a statement of belief in the power of tonality".
   *Works on:* background.
   *How:* not a technique: what he believes of tonality.
   *Where:* [Wikipedia, John Adams]

### Rain at the piano: *China Gates* (1977)

*Added on 27 November 2026, when he was drawn again for composer-blend's piece 78, before it was composed, from an analysis, the article on the piece and a listening guide that reports his programme note.*

7. **Even eighth notes from first bar to last, as steady rain.** *China Gates* is a short piano piece written for the pianist Sarah Cahill, then seventeen, in a rainy season in northern California. He has suggested that its constant eighth notes reflect the steady rain of those weeks; in 2020 he said that the endless winter rain on his small cottage in San Francisco in 1977, with its repeating patterns, inspired it.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *Why:* the rain's repeating patterns are where the piece's patterns come from. [Wikipedia, China Gates; Amarosa] *(his words)*
   *How:* keep one hand in even eighth notes throughout, soft, with no break in the stream.
   *Where:* *China Gates*, the right hand, throughout. [Amarosa; Wikipedia, China Gates]
8. **Two hands in figures of unequal length, drifting in and out of step.** The right hand's figure keeps one length within a gate; the left hand's changes (in the first gate it begins seven eighths long and moves among four, six, seven and eight), so that the hands fall in and out of step. Each hand repeats from two to six different notes in a gate.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *Why:* the analysis reads the drift as the irregularity of the rain that inspired it. [Amarosa] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* give one hand a short figure of fixed length; give the other a figure a little shorter or longer, and change its length now and then. The two coincide only when the lengths are equal or one divides the other.
   *Where:* *China Gates*, the first gate (bars 1 to 15). [Amarosa]
9. **A gate turns the mode on a bass note that stays.** The gates are the moments when the mode changes abruptly; the bass note is the mode's root, and the upper voices change mode over it. In the first part A flat Mixolydian and G sharp Aeolian alternate, nearly the major and the minor on one note: they differ only in their third and sixth degrees. Across a gate each hand keeps at least one of its notes, and the others often move by a half step; each new gate also brings new layers of texture and rhythm.
   *Works on:* harmony, form, texture.
   *Why:* in his programme note, as reported, the piece moves between two modal worlds with great delicacy and asks for attention to the dark, the light and the shadows between them; the analysis hears the major and minor as the rain's gloom and what it makes grow. [Judd; Amarosa] *(his words; an analysis)*
   *How:* hold a low note; above it repeat figures in one mode; at a gate, with no preparation, move two or three of the figure's notes a half step so that the mode turns, keep the others, and change or add a layer.
   *Where:* *China Gates*, the first part; the gate at bars 15 and 16. [Amarosa; Wikipedia, China Gates]
10. **Three parts: two modes, then all four quickly with the bass gone, then two others.** The first part alternates A flat Mixolydian and G sharp Aeolian over their one bass note. In the second (from bar 79) the four modes alternate more quickly, the bass note disappears and the left hand joins the steady eighths. The third (from bar 113) alternates F Lydian and F Locrian over F, two modes that share only two of their notes (F and B).
    *Works on:* form, harmony.
    *How:* a first part on one bass note between a major and a minor mode; a middle with no bass note, changing mode often, both hands in eighths; a last part on another bass note, between its Lydian and its Locrian mode.
    *Where:* *China Gates*, as a whole. [Wikipedia, China Gates; Amarosa]
11. **Almost a palindrome, soft at both ends.** He calls the structure an almost perfect palindrome: the lengths of the gates in the outer parts mirror each other, and the left hand's intervals in each pair of gates are mirror images. The loudness nearly mirrors too: soft, growing to medium loud into the middle part, soft again at bar 135, very soft at 161, and dying away.
    *Works on:* form, loudness.
    *Why:* the analysis hears the three parts as a storm gathering, at its height and passing, with the bass note perhaps as distant thunder. [Amarosa] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* let the second half run the first half's section lengths backwards; soft, louder into the middle, soft again, and let the end die away.
    *Where:* *China Gates*, as a whole. [Amarosa; Wikipedia, China Gates]
12. **For young pianists, without display.** His programme note, as reported: written for young pianists, on the principles of *Phrygian Gates* without its virtuosity.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: what the piece was for.
    *Where:* [Judd]

## Walks through whole works

***Phrygian Gates***, as the article gives it: A Lydian, A Phrygian, E Lydian, E Phrygian, and on round the circle of fifths through half the keys, the Lydian parts shortening. [Wikipedia, Phrygian Gates]

***China Gates***, as the sources give it: a first part, A flat Mixolydian and G sharp Aeolian turning over one bass note (the first gate is bars 1 to 15), soft and growing; a middle part from bar 79, the four modes changing quickly, no bass note, both hands in eighths, medium loud; a last part from bar 113, F Lydian and F Locrian over F, soft at 135, very soft at 161, dying away. [Amarosa; Wikipedia, China Gates]

***Shaker Loops***: four movements, *Shaking and Trembling*, *Hymning Slews*, *Loops and Verses*, *A Final Shaking*. [Wikipedia, Shaker Loops]

## Numbers

- *Phrygian Gates*: 25 to 30 minutes. [Wikipedia, Phrygian Gates]
- *China Gates*: about 4:50. [Wikipedia, China Gates]

## What he does not do

*In* China Gates.

- Break the stream of eighth notes: the right hand keeps it from the first bar to the last.
- Prepare a change of mode: a gate comes at once.

## Too well known to borrow

- ***Short Ride in a Fast Machine***, among his popular works; ***Harmonium*** and ***Harmonielehre***, which first brought him national attention; ***Nixon in China***. [Wikipedia, John Adams]
