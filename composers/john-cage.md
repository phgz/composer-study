## Techniques

1. **A keyboard turned into a percussion band, for a dancer.** *Bacchanale* was written for a choreography by Syvilla Fort in a room too small for percussion instruments but with a grand piano in it. He put objects between the strings (bolts, screws, weather stripping), and the piano became a percussion ensemble for one player. [Wikipedia, prepared piano works]
2. **What a preparation does to a note:** it makes it quieter, gives it another colour, splits it into two or three sounds, and shortens it. The change is meant to be complete, not a tinted piano tone. Each prepared key has its own sound, always the same. [Pearson]
   *How (without a prepared piano):* give each pitch of the piece its own fixed struck sound, and never play that pitch any other way.
3. **Numbers before notes.** A unit of so many bars and a row of proportions are fixed first, and the same proportions cut both the unit into phrases and the whole into sections.
   *Examples:* units of ten bars cut 3, 2, 2, 3 (*Amores*, second movement); eleven sections of eleven bars cut 2, 1, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1 (the Duchamp film music, 1947); seven parts of seven bars; sixteen groups of sixteen bars cut 4, 3, 4, 5; in the first sonata a unit of seven quarters taken 4, 1, 3, 4, 1, 3, 4, 2, 4, 2. In the sonatas the numbers include fractions. [Wikipedia; Pearson]
4. **Two halves, each repeated** (the sonatas). [Pearson]
5. **Few pitches.** A piece's tune on five prepared tones only; a piece of four tones; small collections, often five-note, which can suggest a keynote. [Wikipedia; Pearson]
6. **One line.** Much of it is on one staff, a single line; the texture is sparse and light; chords are the exception. [Wikipedia; Pearson]
7. **Silence built in.** Empty bars alternating with bars of melody (1947, called one of the first systematic explorations of silence); silent bars punctuating the phrases of the second sonata. The gaps count as much as the gestures. [Wikipedia; Pearson]
8. **Short statements, said again at once, never brought back.** Small arch shapes separated by rests; immediate repetition is common, later return is not. Where something is developed it is by repetition, sequence, turning upside down and lengthening (third sonata). [Pearson]
9. **Rhythm against the bar.** Groups placed unpredictably, stresses displaced, the metre left vague; still moments beside quick decorated ones; grace notes. [Pearson]
10. **A treble over an accompaniment that stays put or repeats.** [Pearson]
11. **No chord progressions.** Endings are not made by harmony. [Pearson]
12. **No swell, as a rule.** One piece among those described starts quiet and grows to clusters; the rest keep their level. [Wikipedia]
13. **Behind the *Sonatas and Interludes*:** the eight permanent emotions of Indian aesthetics and their leaning toward tranquillity. [Pearson; Wikipedia]

## What he does not do

*In this music.*

- Progress by harmony, or close with a cadence.
- Bring an earlier idea back later.
- Build to a loud place, with one exception.
