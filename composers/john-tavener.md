# John Tavener (1944-2013)

Begun by Claude on 22 November 2026, from the sources at the end, read that day. It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** The music after his turn to the Orthodox Church in 1977: the choral pieces and *The Protecting Veil*; from 23 November 2026 also the large work for soloists, choir and orchestra *We Shall See Him As He Is*, the handbells of *The Last Sleep of the Virgin*, and, in outline, the late works. **Scores:** none in score-reader's catalogue (all in copyright), so nothing below was checked against notes by me. It rests on other people's analyses and on his own programme notes.

**Measured profile:** none: no work of his is in score-reader's catalogue.

## Techniques

1. **The ison: a note that does not move.** A drone under everything, taken from Byzantine chant; he called it the eternity note.
   *Works on:* harmony, texture.
   *Why:* a sign of timelessness, the divine always there; it also anchors the key and keeps a sound going where there is little accompaniment. [Imrie; Lorenz] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* hold one pitch (or a fifth, a triad, a cluster) for a whole section or a whole piece, and write the tune against it, not chords. Change its scoring from verse to verse (one octave, three octaves, the basses). It may move once, late: rise by step, or sink a third.
   *Where:* *Song for Athene* (throughout), *Magnificat* (on A, scored differently each verse), *The Uncreated Eros* (E-B-E for the whole work), *Nunc Dimittis* (moves from D to B). Not every piece has one: *The Lamb* does not. [Imrie; Lorenz; Wikipedia on *Song for Athene*]

2. **A short tune and its exact mirror, sung together.** The second voice is the first one upside down, interval for interval, note against note. The mirror lands on notes outside the tune's key, so two keys sound at once and half steps rub between the voices.
   *Works on:* melody, harmony.
   *Why:* the strict mirror makes two keys sound at once (in *The Lamb*, G major and E flat major) on one tonal ground; its clashes give brief colour and tension against the smooth, stepwise tune. [Pearson] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a cell of a few notes, by step and third. Give a second voice its exact inversion from the same starting note, in the same rhythm. Then join the notes of both into one longer line; play that line backwards; then the line with its mirror; then both backwards. After that, harmonise the first tune plainly, in four parts, as if nothing had happened.
   *Where:* *The Lamb*: the whole piece is made from its first two bars this way, in seven notes. [Pearson]

3. **Palindromes.** Forms that read the same backwards (ABA, ABCBA), tunes that do, and word orders that do. He took the idea from Webern.
   *Works on:* melody, form.
   *Why:* a symmetrical, circular shape that ends where it began, static as an icon is. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* build the piece outward from its middle; let the last section be the first. A counter-melody can be a palindrome of 49 notes, sung in canon with itself.
   *Where:* *Eonia*, *God is with Us* (ABCBA), *Nunc Dimittis*, *Hymn to the Mother of God* (ABA), the *Magnificat*'s second verse. [Imrie; Lorenz]

4. **Canon as a texture, not as an argument.** Voices follow each other on the same line, sometimes at half speed, and what is heard is a slowly turning chord.
   *Works on:* texture.
   *Why:* a dense, overlapping sound in place of a working-out, and a sense of tradition. [Imrie; Lorenz] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* two inner voices in canon on a four-note motive; around them, the outer voices in canon with every length doubled; space all the voices evenly around the middle pair. Or: two choirs singing the same block chords, one after the other.
   *Where:* *Magnificat*, third verse; *Hymn to the Mother of God*. [Lorenz; Imrie]

5. **Parallel triads in place of harmony.** Whole triads slide up and down with the tune, in root position. Nothing resolves; the chord is a thickening of the line.
   *Works on:* harmony, texture.
   *Why:* moving in parallel, the voices lose their independence and sound as one voice made rich, as an organ stop colours a single note; the harmony comes from the line. [Lorenz; Imrie] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* put a triad under every note of a line. All major, or alternating major and minor. Let a single line split into triads halfway through a phrase. Let two triads a step apart rock back and forth and then close on a third one; repeat a step lower.
   *Where:* the refrains of the *Magnificat* and of the *Ode of St Andrew of Crete*, *Nunc Dimittis*, *Hymn to the Mother of God*. [Imrie; Lorenz]

6. **The same thing in major and in minor, side by side, with no way between.** One or two notes of a tune are changed and it is in another mode. He does not modulate.
   *Works on:* harmony.
   *Why:* the change of mode colours without changing the key: there is no way to modulate, so the change is abrupt. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* state a phrase; state it again with its third (or sixth) changed; put the two next to each other with nothing in between. Or hold a drone and let the chord over it turn from minor to major and back.
   *Where:* *The Uncreated Eros* (one theme in two Byzantine tones by turns), *Eonia* (C minor outside, C major in the middle), *Ode of St Andrew of Crete*. [Imrie]

7. **Blocks, not transitions; refrains; ending where it began.** Sections stand side by side like the flat colours of an icon. A refrain comes back unchanged between verses. Many pieces end on their opening, not on a new place. There is often no climax, or exactly one.
   *Works on:* form.
   *Why:* clear, static structures; ending on the opening, with no climax at the close, is like an icon, which asks nothing of the mind. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* decide the blocks and their order first. Put them side by side with nothing between, or a silence; never a bridge. Bring the refrain back note for note. Put the one loud place late.
   *Where:* *Song for Athene* (six alleluias, each before a text, the loudest place after the sixth, a seventh alleluia to close), *Magnificat*, *Annunciation*. [Wikipedia; Imrie]

8. **Growth by adding voices, not by developing.** The same chord or the same tune again, with more parts.
   *Works on:* texture, form.
   *Why:* the themes are few, so the variety comes from the scoring; in the *Magnificat* the chord growing from three voices to eight is the magnifying of Mary. [Imrie; Lorenz] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* three voices on one triad become eight; a refrain in six parts comes back in thirteen. First statement: one voice over the drone. Second: the same with its mirror, a fourth below. Third: the same, very soft, the triads split over seven parts.
   *Where:* *Magnificat*; *Hymn for the Dormition of the Mother of God*. [Lorenz; Imrie]

9. **Even notes without a metre, a long note at the end of each line.** No time signature; one line of text is one bar; the note values are nearly all the same, and the last of a line is longer. At the end everything is twice as slow.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *Why:* the music follows the words, not a metre ("always guided by the words"); the long notes weigh the ends of the lines, and the whole has the manner of chant. [Pearson; Lorenz] *(his words; an analysis)*
   *How:* six or seven even eighths and a quarter, a line. For the last line, double every length.
   *Where:* *The Lamb*; the *Magnificat* is unmetred too. [Pearson; Lorenz]

10. **A narrow tune.** About six notes of range, by step, with thirds; long arching lines made from the formulas of a mode, not from a scale run. One of his own modes is two identical halves: half step, augmented second, half step, twice.
    *Works on:* melody.
    *Why:* Byzantine melody is built from a mode's formulas, not from a scale, and that gives the tunes their Byzantine character. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* keep a tune within about six notes, moving by step and third, and build long lines from a few repeated turns of the mode; for his mode, use two identical four-note halves (half step, augmented second, half step).
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is* (that mode, on D); the *Magnificat*'s opening motive turns around an augmented second. [Imrie; Lorenz]

11. **A solo instrument as a voice that never stops.** In *The Protecting Veil* the cello is the Mother of God and "never stops singing"; the strings are an extension of her song. Eight continuous sections on the eight Byzantine tones, one of them for the cello alone, the last returning to the first.
    *Works on:* form, colour.
    *How:* give one solo instrument a line that sings almost without a rest from beginning to end, the others extending its song; build the piece from continuous sections, each in its own mode, one for the solo alone, and let the last return to the first.
    *Where:* *The Protecting Veil*, as a whole. [His programme note]

### The larger works, the bells and the late works

*Added on 23 November 2026, when he was drawn for composer-blend's piece 68, from the second half of Imrie's thesis (mostly its analysis of* We Shall See Him As He Is*, for soloists, choir and orchestra), his note on* The Last Sleep of the Virgin *and Wikipedia's account of the late works.*

12. **A halo of held notes.** Divided strings each hold one note of a short rising figure, so that the figure stays sounding as a cluster; later it comes back reversed, then as a palindrome.
    *Works on:* texture, colour.
    *Why:* a shimmering sound that stands for a halo. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* split the strings into five groups; play a five-note rising figure one note at a time, each group holding its note when the next enters, softly; bring the figure back falling, then rising and falling.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, figures 62 and 63 (the "Halo"), reversed in Ikon II, a palindrome in Ikon III. [Imrie]

13. **One blazing major chord after modal music.** The forces join in a tonal D major, held over a D major drone in many parts.
    *Works on:* harmony, loudness, form.
    *Why:* the first climax of the work, which stands apart from the modal music around it. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* after a long stretch in the modes, give everyone one plain major chord, loud and held, over a drone on its keynote; keep it rare.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikon III, around bars 112 and 113 (a D major drone in 24 parts, about 73 parts in all). [Imrie]

14. **Stillness written in.** Whole sections marked to be still: without instruments, "serene and still"; "totally still, petrified", a six-note line in quiet canon; "always as soft as possible" from start to end.
    *Works on:* loudness, texture, form.
    *Why:* peace, an eerie stillness, a floating feeling. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* write a section with no movement but the line itself, at the softest level, and keep it there; a short line in slow canon is enough.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikon II and "The Tomb"; *Eonia* (*sempre pp poss.*). [Imrie]

15. **The edges of the range.** A canon set very low; basses and sopranos at the outer limits of their ranges; a work ending on a violin four octaves above middle C, as soft as possible and without vibrato.
    *Works on:* colour.
    *Why:* the very low canon is "primordial"; the high violin and the massed cellos give a sound the thesis calls distinctly spiritual. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* put a line at the very bottom or the very top of what the voices or instruments can do, softly, and leave it alone there.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikons I, VIII and X. [Imrie]

16. **One theme on more and more of one instrument.** The same theme for four cellos, then seven, then ten.
    *Works on:* colour, texture, form.
    *Why:* the thesis hears the massed cellos as a distinctly spiritual sound. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* bring a theme back several times on the same instrument, each time with more players in unison or in close parts, nothing else changed.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, the Bridal Chamber theme, in Ikons III, V, VII and X. [Imrie]

17. **A climax made by condensing and slowing.** The refrain drawn into unison, its parts reversed, organ and timpani added, its opening third raised, and each statement slower than the last.
    *Works on:* form, loudness, rhythm.
    *Why:* that is what makes Ikon X the climax of the work. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* bring the refrain back several times, each time in fewer parts and more instruments, a little higher at its start and slower.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikon X. [Imrie]

18. **Register as a story.** A solo soprano over a violin drone sings most of her line in the low octave; her last phrase rises to the top.
    *Works on:* melody, form.
    *Why:* the low line is the woman's low standing; the last phrase rising "releases" her. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* keep a solo line low for most of a section, then let its last phrase climb to its highest notes.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikon VI (the Samaritan woman), over a violin drone on D, a compass of two and a half octaves. [Imrie]

19. **Pictures in the instruments, rarely.** Double-bass harmonics and rising thirds for the lame man rising; loud lashing tremolos and unison flourishes for the cleansing of the Temple.
    *Works on:* colour, texture.
    *Why:* each paints its scene. [Imrie] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* where a scene has an action, give it one plain image in the instruments, and drop it when the scene is over.
    *Where:* *We Shall See Him As He Is*, Ikons VII and IV. [Imrie]

20. **Bells that carry the music, at the edge of hearing.** In *The Last Sleep of the Virgin* the handbells hold much of the basic material, and the players are asked to play at the threshold of audibility; tubular bells in *Akathist* and *The Apocalypse*.
    *Works on:* colour, loudness.
    *Why:* in his words the handbells carry the basic material; the very soft playing he tied to his own near-deafness while writing it. In the Orthodox vigil service, the thesis says, handbells take the place of unaccompanied music. [His note on *The Last Sleep*; Imrie] *(his words; an analysis)*
    *How:* give the tune or its core notes to bells and keep the strings around them very soft; let the bells ring out.
    *Where:* *The Last Sleep of the Virgin* (string quartet and handbells, about 20 minutes); *Akathist*; *The Apocalypse*. [His note on *The Last Sleep*; Imrie]

21. **Players placed in the building.** Instruments in galleries and in the dome of a cross-shaped church; a "halo" from the dome; choirs set round the platform by voice.
    *Works on:* colour, form.
    *How:* place groups apart (high, far, on either side) and let the music pass between them.
    *Where:* *Akathist* and others, as the thesis lists them. [Imrie]

22. **Late: instruments from other traditions.** After 2000: ram's horn, ney and kaval in *Fall and Resurrection*; a large Tibetan temple bowl in *Shûnya* and *Towards Silence*; texts from several religions in *The Veil of the Temple*.
    *Works on:* colour, background.
    *Why:* he wanted to be "more universalist"; his Orthodox writing had come to seem too austere to him. [Wikipedia] *(his words)*
    *How:* not a technique on its own: it says that the late works widen the colours, keeping the slow, held, sacred manner.
    *Where:* *Fall and Resurrection* (2000), *Shûnya* (2003), *The Veil of the Temple* (2003), *Towards Silence* (2009). [Wikipedia]

## Walks through whole works

**The Protecting Veil** (cello and strings; from his programme note, which gives the order and the subjects, not bars or times). Eight continuous sections, each on one of the Byzantine tones and one moment of the Mother of God's life: her cosmic beauty over a shattered world; her birth; the Annunciation; the Incarnation; her lament at the cross, the one section for the cello alone, "totally unaccompanied"; the Resurrection; her Falling Asleep; and a last section that evokes her tears. The cello sings throughout; he calls the whole "highly stylised, geometrically formed and meditative". [His programme note]

**We Shall See Him As He Is** (from Imrie's analysis; thirteen movements, eleven of them Ikons). A collage held together by sections that come back changed for each mode: the Bridal Chamber theme in six of the thirteen movements, St John's theme opening most of the Ikons (a solo declamation and its sung echo), the Refrain, and the Halo (technique 12). The first climax is the D major chord of Ikon III (13); Ikon II and "The Tomb" are still (14); Ikon X is the climax of the whole (17) and ends on the high violin (15). [Imrie]

## What he does not do

- **No passage that leads from one section to the next:** contrasting sections stand side by side without a bridge, as the flat areas of colour of an icon do. [Imrie]
- **No progression of chords for its own sake:** the music moves in long lines, and the harmonies are, in the thesis's words, "purely incidental". [Imrie]

## Too well known to borrow

- ***Song for Athene***: his best-known work, sung as Diana, Princess of Wales's coffin left Westminster Abbey in 1997. Its opening (an alleluia sung six times in unison over a held drone) and its seventh alleluia at the end are what a listener knows; technique 7 describes the layout, which can be used without those notes. [Wikipedia, Song for Athene]
