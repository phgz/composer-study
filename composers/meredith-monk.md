# Meredith Monk (born 1942)

Begun by Claude on 25 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 74, from the sources at the end, read on 24 and 25 November 2026; extended the same day for piece 75, when she was chosen again (her own words on rhythm and on how she builds her music, from an interview: 18 and 19). It grows each time she is used.

**Scope so far.** One kind of piece: her wordless vocal music with an ensemble, chiefly the opera *Atlas* (1991), with the songs and ensemble pieces before it (*Dolmen Music*, 1979; *Facing North*, 1990) as two theses and a critic describe them; one line on *Cellular Songs* (2018). **Scores:** none in score-reader's catalogue (she is living; all in copyright), and she teaches much of her music by ear rather than from scores [Pym], so nothing below was checked against notes. What the theses describe from their own transcriptions is given as they give it.

**Measured profile:** none: no work of hers is in score-reader's catalogue.

**The pieces that used this page,** with Philip's words on each: composer-blend, [`reference/composers/meredith-monk.md`](https://github.com/phgz/composer-blend/blob/main/reference/composers/meredith-monk.md) (piece 74: 5, "wow I think this is among the best pieces I've heard so far. Score of 5").

## Techniques

### The voice without words, with an ensemble (*Atlas*, and the music before it)

1. **A two-bar ostinato in 6/8, accented off the beat, under the voice.** In "Home Scene" the piece is essentially in 6/8 over a two-bar ostinato in the winds and piano whose accents fall on the offbeats.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *How:* write a two-bar figure in 6/8 for piano (with winds doubling it), its accents on the weak eighths, and keep it going under every vocal entry.
   *Where:* *Atlas*, Part I, "Home Scene". [Pym]
2. **Two vocal textures at once: a legato line and, above it, a staccato monotone.** In "Home Scene" one texture is a legato melodic line, the other a staccato, accented monotone pitched higher. The mother's monotone begins with four eighths that follow the ostinato and then veers away from it; in the last section she rises to a high E and holds it for four bars.
   *Works on:* melody, texture, rhythm.
   *How:* over the ostinato, give one voice (or the same voice in turn) a smooth line and set above it a single repeated pitch, short and accented; let the monotone first copy the ostinato's rhythm for a beat or two, then go its own way; near the end, lift it to a high note and hold it for four bars.
   *Where:* *Atlas*, Part I, "Home Scene". [Pym]
3. **A slower minor ostinato in one hand, four-bar segments, hollow fifths.** "Loss Song" is also in 6/8, over a slower one-hand piano ostinato in the minor; its vocal segments are four bars each, one of them expanded; parallel fifths give a hollow sound. In it: a breathy monotone with sudden high leaps, a scooped scream falling an octave, a three-note figure repeated, accented grace notes, a phrase rising by step with notes pushed by the breath.
   *Works on:* harmony, form, texture.
   *How:* for a sad scene, slow the ostinato, put it in one hand in the minor; build the voices in four-bar segments and stretch only one; move pairs of voices in parallel fifths.
   *Where:* *Atlas*, Part II, "Loss Song". [Pym]
4. **A gesture sung three times, the third slightly changed; a second voice in near-parallel.** In "Long Shadows (1)" one voice sings a gesture of skips or steps three times at the same pitches and register, with a small rhythmic change at the end of the third; a second voice enters as a countermelody moving largely in parallel at the same speed. Monk asks for singing on an inhaled breath, so that the two lines drift apart by microtones; the second line leaves the pitch set first, and the first follows in the second half of the four-bar phrase.
   *Works on:* melody, texture, form.
   *How:* write a short gesture and repeat it exactly twice; on the third time change only its rhythm at the end. Add a second line in parallel; in the second half of the phrase, let each line in turn step outside the notes used so far.
   *Where:* *Facing North* (1990), "Long Shadows (1)". [Monaghan]
5. **Wide leaps, and an instrument playing the line's mirror.** "Happy Woman" has six leaps in two bars, the largest a tenth; the violin plays a near-complete retrograde inversion of the voice's line, also with six leaps; each part is heard alone, repeated, then the two are layered.
   *Works on:* melody, texture, form.
   *Why:* the thesis notes that an in-tune tenth is rare in operatic vocal writing. [Monaghan] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a two-bar vocal line made almost entirely of leaps; give an instrument the same line backwards and upside down; play each alone, repeat it, then put them together.
   *Where:* *Cellular Songs* (2018), "Happy Woman" (a later kind than *Atlas*). [Monaghan]
6. **A narrow compass, and no change of register without a pause.** Most phrases stay within a very narrow compass whatever the octave; she almost never changes register without a pause; her highest sounds are bright, sometimes wispy, never full, on an "ee" vowel, her bottom register much more open.
   *Works on:* melody, colour.
   *How:* keep each phrase within a few notes; when the voice is to move to another octave, put a rest before it; keep the high notes light, not full.
   *Where:* her songs of the 1970s and early 1980s (*Songs from the Hill*, *Tablet*). [Sandow]
7. **An irregular glide down from a held pitch.** Her most characteristic microtonal device, in the critic's words, is an irregular glide downward from a sustained pitch.
   *Works on:* melody, colour.
   *How:* hold a note, then let it fall, not evenly, to somewhere below it; do not land on a written pitch.
   *Where:* her songs (the critic names no bar). [Sandow]
8. **Modal melodies over a simple keyboard accompaniment.** Her melodies are modal and her accompaniments simple and pop-like; the critic finds the keyboard parts often conventional, and the voice dovetailing with them too predictably.
   *Works on:* harmony, melody.
   *How:* keep the keyboard to a few chords in a repeating pattern, the voice in a mode over it.
   *Where:* the songs with keyboard (*Our Lady of Late*, *Tablet*). [Sandow]
9. **Two keys at once in the keyboard: A flat major fifths over F major octaves.** The "Spirit Music" of "Future Quest" has no voice; it moves in the guitar's rhythm, with A flat major in parallel fifths in the right hand against F major in parallel octaves below. The left hand's octaves rise F, G, A where the guitar's line had fallen D, C, B flat.
   *Works on:* harmony.
   *Why:* the thesis reads the rising octaves as the resolution of the heroine's conflict, and the two keys at once as a sign that can be heard either way. [Monaghan] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* in the right hand, parallel fifths in A flat major; in the left, octaves in F major, moving in the same rhythm; let the bass rise by step where an earlier line fell.
   *Where:* *Atlas*, Part I, "Future Quest", bars 13 to 21. [Monaghan]
10. **A tune that leaves a note out, and one that reaches short and then corrects itself.** Neither of the heroine's daydream tunes in "Future Quest" contains a B or a B flat. Later the mother's new two-bar phrase, in F major, leaps first only to B flat instead of C; its second bar reaches C, then falls back to B flat.
    *Works on:* melody.
    *Why:* the thesis reads the mother's corrected leap as her resolve to let her daughter go. [Monaghan] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* write a tune that avoids one degree of the scale entirely; when that degree finally appears, let a leap stop on it a step short of where the ear expects, and reach the expected note only the next time.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, "Future Quest", the daydream tunes (from bar 21 and from bar 49) and bars 89 to 92. [Monaghan]
11. **Everyone's theme at once, to the scene's climax.** In "Future Quest" all the characters sing their themes together (bars 73 to 80), building to a circle walk and the heroine's jump.
    *Works on:* texture, form.
    *How:* after each theme has been heard alone, put them all together, unchanged, for eight bars, at the scene's high point.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, "Future Quest", bars 73 to 80. [Monaghan]
12. **A self-hocket, then a hocket between singers.** The heroine sings a "self-hocket" across two registers (from bar 7 of "Explorers' Procession"); the guides then sing it as a true hocket, the parts split between them.
    *Works on:* texture, rhythm.
    *How:* write a line that jumps between two registers note by note; then give its high notes to one singer and its low notes to another.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, "Explorers' Procession", from bar 7. [Monaghan]
13. **Rapid syllables traded between groups; the end in unison across octaves.** *Dolmen Music* is for three women, three men and cello: the women open on a rising fourth, low for their voices; the men answer in unison a semitone above; the two groups layer their melodies, trade rapid syllables without language, and end in unison, in octaves.
    *Works on:* texture, form.
    *How:* two groups, each in unison; let the second answer the first a half step off; layer them; trade quick syllables; close with everyone on one line in octaves.
    *Where:* *Dolmen Music* (1979). [Monaghan]
14. **Sounds beyond singing, each with a dramatic role.** A "high chirp" alternating sharp timbres on eighths and rests (the heroine's first, "Choosing Companions", bar 43); a "chirp conversation" of vocal fry, scream, low growl and goat trill for the ice demons; laughs, sobs, whoops, sirens and stuttering over a synthesized ostinato in "Treachery". The goat trill means danger in *Atlas*, old age in *Education of the Girlchild*.
    *Works on:* colour.
    *Why:* in the thesis's reading, the characters who change most have the widest vocabularies of sounds. [Monaghan] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* not a recipe for an instrument: these are sung sounds, given to a character as the music's sign of who it is.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, "Choosing Companions", "Ice Demons", "Treachery (Temptation)". [Monaghan; Pym]
15. **A held vowel falling a minor third, the second syllable almost lost.** In "Choosing Companions" a sustained vowel leads into a falling minor third so that the second syllable is almost inaudible; later, accents on the consonant and on diphthongs turn the same syllables into an excited exclamation.
    *Works on:* melody, colour.
    *How:* hold a note, fall a minor third and let the lower note fade at once; later sing the same two notes short and accented.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, "Choosing Companions". [Monaghan]
16. **Accents that shift phase, tapped by feet.** In "Guides' Dance" the guides tap in wooden sandals with accents that shift phase; their first sounds are not vocal at all.
    *Works on:* rhythm.
    *How:* two or more players tap the same pattern, one moving its accent by a step each time round.
    *Where:* *Atlas*, Part II, "Guides' Dance". [Monaghan]
17. **The voice as an instrument that needs no words.** Her own account: the voice could have the flexibility of the spine and the articulation of a hand; it did not need words, and was an eloquent language in itself; she became interested in non-verbal, abstract song forms. On *Dolmen Music*'s sleeve she says she has developed a vocabulary meant to use as wide a range of vocal sounds as possible.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: why her songs have no words. Each sound is chosen as an instrumentalist chooses a stroke.
    *Where:* her writing and interviews. [Monaghan; Sandow] *(her words)*

18. **Rhythm as the ground; time compressed, stretched, twisted, interrupted.** In her own account, rhythm comes first: she calls it "the ground base of everything I do", from her training as a musician, and says of time that she can "compress it, extend it, twist it, interrupt it". She built her first movement vocabulary from her own body's rhythms and impulses, and then worked with her voice as she had with her body.
    *Works on:* rhythm, form.
    *Why:* her words: being trained as a musician made her "very sensitive to rhythm", and that is the ground of all she does. [Jowitt] *(her words)*
    *How:* take one rhythmic figure and make the piece by what is done to its time: the same figure shortened, drawn out, its accent turned, broken off; the body's rhythm and the voice's treated as one.
    *Where:* her work in general, as she describes it in the interview. [Jowitt]

19. **An additive texture: one layer at a time, each thickening the last.** For *16 Millimeter Earrings* she describes "an additive sound environment" of live vocal phrases, text and sound, which set off tape loops of fragments of the live performance (four tape recorders were used), the sound growing "thicker and richer" as each layer was added; covering, or layering, was the piece's basic principle. She brings singers taped or written phrases and fixes the music in rehearsal by memory of the muscles rather than reading.
    *Works on:* texture, form.
    *Why:* her words: layering was "a basic principle of the piece". [Jowitt] *(her words)*
    *How:* begin with one layer; add the next over it without taking the first away; let the texture's growth be the form.
    *Where:* *16 Millimeter Earrings*. [Jowitt]

## Walks through whole works

***Atlas*, "Future Quest (The Call)"** (Part I, 11:15 on the recording; from the thesis's reading, partly from its own transcription). The "Spirit Music" (bars 13 to 21), no voice, A flat major fifths over F major octaves (9); the heroine's daydream tune, a two-bar arpeggio in F major sung twice with glides between "la", "ah" and humming, first over her own guitar (D minor, A major, B flat minor seventh, D minor), then from bar 21 over keyboard chords and cello; the Spirit Music once more (bars 29 to 33), a clarinet in D Dorian over it from bar 33; from bar 47 the harmony moving between G minor and A major without cadencing on D minor; a second daydream (from bar 49) filled with fast stepwise runs and more syllables, still without any B; the father's "No" theme (bar 65), in D minor; every character's theme at once (bars 73 to 80, 11) to a circle walk and her jump; the mother's corrected leap (bars 89 to 92, 10); the heroine's theme broken into wide leaps and quintuplets (bars 93 to 97); the mother's variation in runs of twelve (bars 98 and 103), an E flat major chord turning the orchestra to C Dorian; the father's theme inverted (bar 106); the second daydream again over C Mixolydian, the parents holding a unison C, the close on C Mixolydian leaning towards F (bars 114 to 121). [Monaghan; Wikipedia, Atlas]

## Numbers

- ***Atlas*** (1991): an opera in three parts (*Personal Climate*, *Night Travel*, *Invisible Light*); the recording (ECM, 1993) lasts 128 minutes, "Future Quest" 11:15, "Agricultural Community" 14:36. Forces on the recording: many voices; cello, viola, violin; bass clarinet, horn, shawm; glass harmonica, sheng, keyboards, sampler, percussion. [Wikipedia, Atlas]
- **"Home Scene"** and **"Loss Song":** 6/8; "Loss Song" in four-bar segments. [Pym]
- **"Agricultural Community":** contrapuntal vocal lines on syllables without language for 38 bars. [Monaghan]
- ***Dolmen Music*** (1979): six voices and cello. [Monaghan]

## What she does not do

*In the music these sources describe.*

- **No words:** the syllables are not a language [Monaghan; Pym].
- **Rarely a written score:** she teaches her works by ear, and the thesis holds that what matters in them cannot really be written down [Pym].
- **Little change of register without a pause** [Sandow].
