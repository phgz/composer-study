## Techniques

### The bells in his piano preludes

1. **The toll: three low notes, one to a beat, each in three octaves.** The C sharp minor prelude opens with A, G sharp, C sharp: down a semitone, then down a fifth to the keynote, the lowest note of the piano's bottom octave.
   *Works on:* melody, colour.
   *Why:* bells: the slow outer sections bring Russian Orthodox bell-ringing into the piano, the sound of Russia in his early music. [Frolova-Walker] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* three slow strokes, one to a beat, each a single note doubled in three octaves at the bottom of the keyboard, ending on the keynote; nothing else sounds with them. Use your own three notes: these three are his best-known figure.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 3 no. 2, bars 1 and 2. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]**
2. **The three notes go on underneath as the bass,** over and over: thirteen times on those same notes, seventeen with the same shape moved to other notes.
   *Works on:* harmony, texture.
   *Why:* the same three notes stay under everything, so the toll is never gone while the chords move above it. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* after the toll has been heard alone, keep it as the bass, one note to a beat, under whatever comes above; move its shape to other notes now and then, and come back to the first three.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 3 no. 2, the bass in bars 1 to 19 and 46 to 53. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]**
3. **Each low note is answered off the beat by a thick chord higher up:** stroke on the beat, six-note chord on the half-beat, three notes to a hand, in the middle of the keyboard. The chords' top line moves by step, mostly downward.
   *Works on:* harmony, rhythm, texture.
   *Why:* the low note and the chord never begin together: each stroke is heard alone first, and the chord comes into its ringing. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* between the bass strokes, put a chord of six notes (two hands, three each) on every half-beat, in the middle of the keyboard; let the top notes of the chords make a line that steps down.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 3 no. 2, bars 3 to 8. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]**
4. **When it comes back it is the same music, thicker:** the chords have eight notes, twice eleven, and it is loud; then it dies away on soft chords of the home key. The middle is fast (three times the speed) in running triplets.
   *Works on:* texture, form, loudness.
   *How:* make a slow, quiet section, a fast middle in running triplets, and then the first section again with the same notes doubled into chords of eight notes or more and loud, dying away at the end on the home chord.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 3 no. 2: slow, bars 1 to 13; fast, 14 to 42; slow and thick, 43 to 61. **[score: op. 3 no. 2]**
5. **The peal: high notes coming down by step, each alternating with one fixed pair of notes.** In the B flat prelude the right hand has A, G, F in octaves, and between each of them the same B flat and D: bells of different sizes against one that does not change. Before it, a short fanfare: a quick upbeat and a chord.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm, texture.
   *Why:* the peal is joined to a fanfare, as in Rimsky-Korsakov's *Kitezh*. [Frolova-Walker] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* high in the right hand, a line coming down by step in octaves, and after each of its notes the same pair of notes struck again, so that one bell stays while the others move. Start it with a quick upbeat and a chord.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 23 no. 2, from bar 3. **[score: op. 23 no. 2]**
6. **Under the peal the bass rolls:** a low octave on the beat, then a broken chord up two and a half octaves and down again, six notes to the beat, every beat.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *How:* in the left hand, every beat: a low octave, then the notes of the chord going up and coming down, six to the beat, without a break.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 23 no. 2, from bar 1. **[score: op. 23 no. 2]**
7. **The small bell that swings:** in the B minor prelude, between slow chords low and middle, two pairs of notes rock in a long-short rhythm, the same two pairs again and again.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *How:* between slow chords, two pairs of notes, each pair a sixth or a third apart, alternating in a long-short rhythm late in the bar, repeated unchanged.
   *Where:* Prelude op. 32 no. 10, bars 1 to 6. **[score: op. 32 no. 10]**
8. **His tunes move by step and circle a few notes,** in the manner of church chant; several preludes each favour one interval (fifths, thirds, seconds, fourths).
   *Works on:* melody.
   *Why:* his tunes come from Russian Orthodox chant, which he took so deeply into his language that it is everywhere in it; one interval per prelude ties the tune to the figures around it (in the B flat prelude the fourth is both the tune's call and the accompaniment's shape). [Frolova-Walker] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a tune that moves mostly by step within a narrow span and keeps coming back to one note; give a whole piece one favourite interval and let it shape the tune and the chords.
   *Where:* the lecture names the G flat major prelude (fifths), the A minor (thirds), the B minor (seconds) and the B flat major (fourths). [Frolova-Walker]
9. **The Dies irae,** the chant for the dead, comes all through his work as a sign of death; in the B minor prelude it is not quoted but nearly.
   *Works on:* melody.
   *How:* where a piece turns to death, let a line come close to the chant's opening shape (stepwise, narrow, falling) without quoting it.
   *Where:* the B minor prelude (op. 32 no. 10), by the source; no bar is named. [Frolova-Walker]
10. **How the bells he heard are rung** (not his technique, his model): they are not tuned to a scale and play no melody; the ringing is rhythm and the mix of colours. The big bell tolls slowly and alone to call (three slow strokes, then steadier ones). For the dead, each bell is struck once from the smallest to the largest, each stroke left to fade, and then all together. For a feast, all the bells ring at once, in a pattern said three times.
    *Works on:* rhythm, colour.
    *How:* for a call, one low bell alone, three slow strokes and then steadier ones. For a death, single strokes from high to low, each left to die, then all together. For a feast, every bell at once in a rhythmic pattern, said three times with short pauses.
    *Where:* the Russian Orthodox practice: the calling toll, the funeral toll and the feast peal. [Wikipedia, bell ringing]

### The piano concertos (Nos. 2 and 3, from sources)

11. **Bells before the tune.** The Second Concerto opens with the piano alone, tolling chords that build tension, until the main theme arrives in the violins, violas and first clarinet.
    *Works on:* form, colour.
    *How:* begin with the soloist alone, low chords struck one after another and growing; let the tune enter in the orchestra at their height.
    *Where:* Piano Concerto No. 2, first movement. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 2]
12. **The orchestra sings, the soloist accompanies,** with quick arpeggios rocking between the hands; the soloist takes the tune only later, when it comes back.
    *Works on:* texture.
    *How:* give the first statement of the tune to the strings and a clarinet, and the piano rapid broken chords under it; at the return, the piano plays the tune.
    *Where:* Piano Concerto No. 2, first movement, exposition and recapitulation. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 2]
13. **The slow movement: strings, then a simple figure on the piano, then the tune in a wind solo;** a short climax centred on the piano leads to a cadenza, and the movement ends with the soloist alone.
    *Works on:* form, colour.
    *How:* open with slow chords in the strings; let the piano enter with a plain broken-chord figure; give the tune to the flute, then a long clarinet solo; build briefly to the piano, let it play alone (a cadenza), and close with it alone, softly.
    *Where:* Piano Concerto No. 2, second movement. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 2]
14. **A plain tune that turns into figuration** (he said it "wrote itself"); after the cadenza, the winds take the first theme one after another.
    *Works on:* melody, texture.
    *How:* state a simple, stepwise tune; let the piano soon spin it into busy figures; after the solo, pass it from flute to oboe to clarinet to horn.
    *Where:* Piano Concerto No. 3, first movement. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 3]
15. **The last tune loud, at the end, in the major;** and a closing four-note rhythm that some call his signature.
    *Works on:* form, rhythm.
    *How:* end the last movement with its lyrical second theme, loud and in the major; close on four strokes in a fixed rhythm.
    *Where:* Piano Concerto No. 3, finale; No. 2 ends "very triumphantly" in the major. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 3; No. 2]
16. **What the concertos are known for:** memorable, songful melodies in every movement, lyrical themes, music that "sounds as if it wrote itself" (Stephen Hough, quoted).
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: what listeners and writers find in them.
    *Where:* [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 2]

## What he does not do

*In these three.*

- Leave the bass out: there is a low note or a low chord in nearly every bar.
- Put the answering chords on the beat with the low strokes.
- Decorate the toll: the three notes are bare octaves.

## Too well known to borrow

- **The Prelude in C sharp minor op. 3 no. 2**, and its opening three-note toll above all: one of his most famous works, demanded as an encore ("C-sharp!") until he called it his "Frankenstein"; heard in many films, sampled in pop songs, arranged for jazz. Technique 1 is its toll: take three low strokes of your own (composer-blend's piece 67 first took his and changed them). [Wikipedia, Prelude]

- **Piano Concertos Nos. 2 and 3:** No. 2 "one of his most enduringly popular pieces", first in a radio station's yearly poll of favourites three years running; No. 3 seen as "the pinnacle" of his career, its fame spread by Horowitz's recording. Techniques 11 to 15 are what they do, to take without their tunes. [Wikipedia, Piano Concerto No. 2; No. 3]
