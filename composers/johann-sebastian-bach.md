# Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750)

Begun by Claude on 28 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 84 (the tune made of the holes). It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** Two kinds of piece. The dances of his suites and partitas for one instrument (the suites BWV 1007 to 1012, the partitas BWV 1002, 1004, 1006 and 1013): their courantes, gigues and bourrées, 22 movements. And the six Schübler chorales for organ, BWV 645 to 650 (published 1747 or 1748), for how a slow chorale tune sits inside quicker parts. **Scores:** yes. score-reader's catalogue holds 959 of his works; the 22 dances were read in the DCML analysts' edition, the six chorales in Mutopia's. The readings are composer-study's `checks/readings-for-84-2026-11-28/`.

## Techniques

### The dances for one instrument

1. **The dance in one even stream of notes.** In his courantes nearly every note is as long as the one before: 84% over the 15 courantes and correntes read, 99% in the Corrente of the partita BWV 1002, 97% in the courantes of the suites BWV 1008 and 1009; 80% over the seven gigues. The profile sets his solo courantes apart from other composers' courantes by it (those it dates to Weimar: 84% against 52%).
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *Why:* with the rhythm held even, the time of every next note is given and only its pitch is news: the profile measures his solo courantes' rhythm as 0.76 bits of surprise a note against 1.46 in other composers' courantes. **[score: the profile, courante (solo instrument), Weimar]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* write the dance in one note value from start to end (eighths in a courante, sixteenths in a quick one), changing it only at a close.
   *Where:* BWV 1002, Corrente; BWV 1008 and 1009, Courante. **[score: the 22 dances]**
2. **A held note inside the line.** Where one line carries two, it is nearly always one pitch coming back between moving notes. In the 22 dances, 2.9% of the notes fall in runs of six or more in which the line leaps up and down at every note while each of its two strands moves by a step or less; in 2.7%, one of the two strands keeps a single pitch. The longest: the Gigue of the suite BWV 1009, from bar 25, 42 notes, every other one the same.
   *Works on:* texture, melody.
   *Why:* the returning pitch stands for a held note, and the notes between its returns, moving by step, are a line of their own: two parts from one instrument, every other note each. **[score: BWV 1009, Gigue]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* for a few bars, alternate one fixed note with a line moving by step, a note each.
   *Where:* BWV 1009, Gigue, bar 25; BWV 1012, Gigue, bar 64; BWV 1013, Corrente, bar 50; BWV 1008, Courante, bar 25. **[score: the 22 dances]**
3. **The courante's turn.** Down a step, up a step, down a step, in even notes (B3, A3, B3, A3): his solo courantes use it 6.2 times in 1,000 four-note figures, the other composers' courantes not at all.
   *Works on:* melody.
   *How:* rock between two notes a step apart, in the dance's even notes, before the line moves on.
   *Where:* BWV 1012, Courante, bar 14; BWV 1007, Courante, bar 5; BWV 1010, Courante, bar 23. **[score: the profile, courante (solo instrument), Weimar]**

### The Schübler chorales (BWV 645 to 650)

4. **The chorale let in phrase by phrase.** In each of the six, the quick parts begin alone, and the chorale tune first comes in at bar 5 to 15 (the edition's numbering). It comes in 2 to 10 phrases with the quick parts going on between them, so that it sounds only 30% to 35% of the time, while the quick parts sound 98% to 100% of it. Its notes are two to eight times as long as theirs (a median of one or two quarters, against a quarter or a half of one).
   *Works on:* texture, form, melody.
   *Why:* the quick parts never stop, so the chorale is a second, slower layer that comes and goes over one unbroken stream. **[score: BWV 645 to 650]** *(from the score)*
   *How:* write the quick parts as a whole piece first; then lay the tune's phrases into it in long notes, one at a time, with bars of the quick parts alone before the first and between each.
   *Where:* BWV 645, the first phrase at bar 13 (10 phrases); 646, bar 6 (6); 647, bar 5 (6); 648, bar 9 (2); 649, bar 15 (4); 650, bar 13 (4). **[score: BWV 645 to 650]**
5. **One of the lines is the plain tune.** All six are chorale fantasias: one melody line is the chorale tune, hardly ornamented (a cantus firmus). Five are note-for-note transcriptions of movements of his church cantatas, the chorale cantatas; BWV 650 is the second movement of cantata 137. The two in the middle of the set (647, 648) are in four parts, the others trios.
   *Works on:* background.
   *How:* not a technique beyond 4: what the set is.
   *Where:* BWV 645 to 650. [Wikipedia, Schübler Chorales]

## Walks through whole works

**Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme, BWV 645** (from the score): the quick line and the bass alone until the chorale's first phrase, at bar 13 (the edition's numbering); then its others, ten phrases of 4 to 13 notes (75 notes in all), the quick parts going on between them; the chorale sounds 35% of the movement's 220.5 quarters, the others 100%.

## What he does not do

*In the 22 dances read.*

- Split one line into two moving strands for long: a run that leaps at every note almost always keeps one strand on one pitch (2.7% of the notes of the 2.9%).

## Not checked

- The run detector's rule is the writer's (every move a leap of a fourth or more, alternating up and down; each strand by a step or less): a line that carries two in a looser way (the cello suites' preludes, broken chords) is not counted, and the preludes were not read.
- Which voice of the cantata sings the chorale in BWV 645's model, and the other cantata models but 650's: the sources read disagree or were not read.
- That the chorale's line in each Schübler chorale is the part with the fewest notes: chosen so by the reading, and right where checked by its long notes; Mutopia's parts were read as its edition names them, a doubled staff once.
- The dates: the catalogue's (the profile's Köthen and Weimar).
- The instruments of the suites and partitas (Claude believes the suites BWV 1007 to 1012 are for cello, the partitas BWV 1002, 1004 and 1006 for violin, BWV 1013 for flute): the catalogue says "solo instrument", and no source read for the page names them.
