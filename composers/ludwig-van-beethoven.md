## Techniques

1. **A hymn in the Lydian mode.** F with B natural where F major has B flat. In the first hymn (30 bars, 309 notes) there is not one B flat: the notes are the white keys, and one C sharp. He wrote the mode into the title. **[score; Chamber Music Society]**
2. **Two note values.** In the first hymn every note is a half note (162) or a quarter (147). The half notes are the hymn. **[score]**
3. **A short introduction before every line.** Two bars of quarters in which the four enter one after another, *sotto voce*: first violin, second violin, viola, cello, each half a bar after the one before. Then the line: eight half notes, four bars, the four together in block chords. Five times: four lines, and the last line again with a new ending. **[score; Chamber Music Society]**
   *How:* 2 bars of quarters, the parts entering every 2 quarters on one small figure, then 4 bars of chords in equal half notes; repeat for each line.
4. **Each line swells.** A crescendo is marked in every line, and the hymn is *piano* with *forte* four times. No ornament anywhere in the three hymns. **[score]**
5. **It alternates with "feeling new strength."** *Andante*, 3/8, in D major, 53 bars both times. Quick decoration: in the first, 513 of 1,063 notes are thirty-seconds. Twelve trills each time. The first violin leaps up to two and a half octaves. Soft and loud stand side by side (*p* 46 times, *f* 23, *pp* 11). **[score]**
6. **He changes keynote for it, not only mode:** from F to D, a third below, and from no sharps to two. **[score]**
7. **Three hymns, each changed.** A theme and two variations. [Chamber Music Society]
   - *Second:* the first violin alone has the long notes (41 half notes and longer); second violin and viola move under it in quarters, eighths and dotted quarters; the cello has one long note in 31 bars. **[score]**
   - *Third* (*Mit innigster Empfindung*, with the most inward feeling): the long notes are shared by all four again (44 to 49 each), with eighths and sixteenths around them. The hymn is pared down and the introductions and the hymn grow together. **[score; Wikipedia]**
8. **One loud place, late.** The hymns stay soft until the third, where 36 *sforzando* marks fall in bars 188 to 193; then *pianissimo* to the end. **[score]**
9. **The hymns grow, the other sections do not.** In quarter notes: 122, 79.5, 124, 79.5, 172. **[score]**
10. **Why the style.** The slow block chords imitate how hymns were sung in church then, very slowly. He had harmonised the plainsong *Tantum ergo* in 1820, while at work on the *Missa solemnis*. The Lydian mode was sometimes linked with healing. [Chamber Music Society]
11. **Why the piece.** He wrote it after an intestinal illness that lasted through the winter of 1824-25 and that he had feared would kill him. The title: holy song of thanks of a convalescent to the Deity. [Wikipedia]

## Walks through whole works

**Op. 132, third movement** (211 bars; 15 to 20 minutes). Hymn, *Molto adagio*, 4/4, bars 1 to 30: five times an introduction and a line. *Neue Kraft fühlend*, *Andante*, 3/8, D major, bars 31 to 83. Hymn again, bars 84 to 114: the tune in the first violin over moving parts. *Andante* again, bars 115 to 167, more decorated (665 thirty-seconds), with a few plucked bars. Hymn, *Mit innigster Empfindung*, bars 168 to 211: pieces of the first line passed among the four, the loud place at 188 to 193, the end very soft.

## What he does not do

*In this movement.*

- Use B flat in the first hymn, or any ornament in a hymn.
- Keep the keynote when the dance comes.
- Make the hymn loud before its third time.
