## Techniques

### The repetitions: process music and *Einstein on the Beach*

1. **Music with repetitive structures, not music that repeats.** His own preferred description is "music with repetitive structures"; repetition in his sense "is a process of constant repositioning and rearrangement", and an extreme economy of material makes a web of associations between the motifs.
   *Works on:* form, background.
   *How:* treat each repetition as a step that moves something (a note added, a group regrouped, a chord kept and one note changed), never as a copy.
   *Where:* the whole output; shown in *Strung Out* (1967 or 1969) and String Quartet No. 6. [Liberatore]
2. **The additive process: a figure repeated, a note added.** A bar with a group of notes is repeated several times; a note is added to the group, then another, and so on; and the same can run backwards (the subtractive process). His words: "A simple figure can expand and then contract in many different ways, maintaining the same general melodic configuration but, because of the addition of one note, it takes on a very different rhythmic shape." Its origins are given as Indian music, which he studied with Ravi Shankar.
   *Works on:* rhythm, form, melody.
   *Why:* one note added keeps the shape of the figure and changes its rhythm (his words, from *Music by Philip Glass*, as quoted) [Brown University course page] *(his words)*
   *How:* write a short figure; play it several times; play it again with one note more (or one less); keep going, each version repeated before the next.
   *Where:* *Music in Fifths* (1969), "one of dozens of steps in an additive process"; *Two Pages* (1968 or 1969); *Music in Similar Motion* (1969), where voices are also added at fixed points. [Puget Sound, *Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom*; Brown University course page; Rubin, "Process and Design in *Music in Similar Motion*"; classical-music.com]
3. **A chord progression lengthened chord by chord.** In *Einstein on the Beach*, Act 1 scene 1 ("Train"), "each chord in a five-chord cadential progression is gradually lengthened by adding to the number of subdivisions given to each chord."
   *Works on:* rhythm, harmony.
   *How:* take a short progression and play it round and round; each time round, give one chord (or each chord) one more beat, so the cycle stretches while its order stays.
   *Where:* *Einstein on the Beach*, "Train". [Puget Sound]
4. **Two small gestures as the seed of a whole piece.** In the violin solo *Strung Out* a five-note group in two parts seeds the entire thirteen minutes; in the second grouping "the third repeats, and the descending scale truncates after two notes". The music "seems to spiral outward, turning back on itself, while never returning to where it started."
   *Works on:* melody, form.
   *Why:* an economy of material that makes every later figure heard against the first [Liberatore] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write one figure of five notes in two parts; make every later figure by repeating, dropping or truncating a part of it; never play the first figure exactly again.
   *Where:* *Strung Out*, the opening (Liberatore's Example 1). [Liberatore]
5. **Familiar chords, a progression that keeps a note and goes nowhere.** His harmony is "the most vernacular vocabulary: major and minor triads and seventh chords", arranged in a way of his own: in *Knee Play 2* (line 3) each bar spells a triad or a familiar chord, "Each harmonic shift retains something of the previous chord", the voice-leading moves by small steps "but intriguingly directionless", and the progression is five bars long, then repeats; the uneven length adds to the ambiguity.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *Why:* "This unusual phrase length augments the ambiguity of the harmonic progression" [Liberatore] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a cycle of five chords, each a triad or seventh, each keeping at least one note of the chord before and moving the others by step, with no dominant pull towards the first; repeat it.
   *Where:* *Einstein on the Beach*, *Knee Play 2*, line 3 (Liberatore's Examples 3a and 3b). [Liberatore]
6. **Cadential progressions as the opera's recurring cycles.** In *Einstein* "the famous five-chord progression modulates from F minor to E major" and is the basis of the Spaceship scene and of the C sections of "Train"; the three-chord progression of the first and last Knee Plays "seems cadential as well: a simple vi-V-I."
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *How:* make a few short progressions that each end like a cadence, and let them come back as the recurring blocks of a long work, each scene built on one.
   *Where:* *Einstein on the Beach*: the first and last Knee Plays, "Train", "Spaceship". [Gann]
7. **Modules recombined, not by arithmetic.** In "Building" four modules make the form by their changing combinations: one is added, then another, "then steps 2 and 3 are added together"; after growing by additions of A the music strips back to B and D, then C is added. "The move from one pattern to another, however, is not at all predictable"; "Train"'s third section is recomposed by intuition, its ending "a subtractive rhythmic process of 5-4-3-2".
   *Works on:* form, rhythm.
   *Why:* "This possibility of intuitively recomposing a section is far removed from the typical concept of early minimalism" [Gann] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write four short modules; build the form from their combinations, adding and dropping one at a time, but choose the next combination by ear, not by a count; end by shortening the groups, five, four, three, two.
   *Where:* *Einstein on the Beach*, "Building" and "Train". [Gann]

### The string quartets

8. **Layered counts that meet only on the downbeat.** In String Quartet No. 6 (2013), bars 1 to 5: the second violin plays four even quarters a bar; the viola and cello divide the bar's eight eighths 3+3+2; the first violin plays three groups of two; "the downbeat is the only point in each measure in which everyone plays together." In bar 4 the first violin shifts to two groups of three while the lower strings lock into quarters; in bar 5 the players switch roles, and the cello adds four groups of two.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *How:* give each instrument its own grouping of the same bar (4, 3+3+2, 2+2+2), all meeting on beat one; after a few bars move one player to another grouping, then let them trade groupings.
   *Where:* String Quartet No. 6, bars 1 to 5 (Liberatore's Example 2). [Liberatore]
9. **Four short movements, slow and fast by turns, on one theme in arpeggios.** String Quartet No. 2, *Company* (1983), music for a stage version of Beckett's prose *Company*: four movements titled only by their metronome marks, quarter = 96, 160, 96, 160, about seven to nine minutes in all. "The main theme of this work is subjugated to arpeggios in minor keys all along the four movements"; the movements are closely related, a "monochrome" work.
   *Works on:* form, colour.
   *Why:* the quartet allowed "both an introspective and passionate quality well suited to the text"; Beckett chose four places in the text, his "interstices as it were", and the four short pieces "turned out to be a thematically cohesive work" [Glass's programme note, Wise Music] *(his words)*
   *How:* write four short movements in two tempos by turns, each built on arpeggios of minor chords carrying one theme, so that the four sound like one thing seen four times.
   *Where:* String Quartet No. 2 *Company*. [Wikipedia, String Quartet No. 2 (Glass); Wise Music Classical; Hollywood Bowl; the Listeners' Club]
10. **A Romantic opening, then the pulse; at the end the opening's mood again.** String Quartet No. 5 (1991, for the Kronos Quartet), in five movements: the first, elegiac, "sounds like late Romanticism: sweeping, pensive, elegiac" and is a short prelude to the second, "where the insistent pulse and repeating figures kick in"; the finale's unison scales rise and fall "before subsiding into a reminiscence of the quartet's opening mood." His own description: a "quartet about musicality".
    *Works on:* form, colour.
    *How:* open with a slow, singing prelude in a Romantic manner; let the pulse and the repeating figures take over from the second movement on; end the last movement by falling back into the first's mood.
    *Where:* String Quartet No. 5. [Apple Music Classical's note; IRCAM's catalogue entry]

## What he does not do

*In the passages the sources analyse.*

- **Lead the harmony anywhere:** the voice-leading of *Knee Play 2* is smooth "but intriguingly directionless" [Liberatore].
- **Keep to the arithmetic:** in "Building" the sequence of patterns is "very unpredictable", whatever his comment about "simple arithmetic progressions" [Gann].
