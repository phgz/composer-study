# Mieczysław Weinberg (1919-1996)

Begun by Claude on 23 November 2026. It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** Three things: the child's song that darkens (the Children's Songs op. 13), the solo line over a bare accompaniment (the Clarinet Sonata op. 28), and the devices of his string quartets, from the Sixth (1946) on (added on 24 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 72). **Scores:** none read. His music is under copyright; this page is from eight sources, and what they say is said here in other words.

**Measured profile:** none: no work of his is in score-reader's catalogue.

**The pieces that used this page,** with Philip's words on each: composer-blend, [`reference/composers/mieczyslaw-weinberg.md`](https://github.com/phgz/composer-blend/blob/main/reference/composers/mieczyslaw-weinberg.md).

## Techniques

1. **A wordless song at the start, and the same song at the end, sadder.** The Children's Songs open with an introduction sung without words; the coda repeats its material in a much sadder tone, after the cycle has gone from four light, playful songs to a child's grief at a home destroyed.
   *Works on:* melody, form.
   *Why:* the return, sadder, is a lament for what has been lost (the review's phrase is the land of lost content). [MusicWeb] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* open with a short wordless tune (on a syllable, or on an instrument); at the very end bring it back with the same notes, slower, softer and in a darker harmony, after what came between has changed its meaning.
   *Where:* Children's Songs op. 13, the introduction and the coda. [MusicWeb; Oxford Song]
2. **From light to darkness,** by sharp contrast: lyrical and scherzo-like songs against tragic ones; the war through a child's eyes.
   *Works on:* form.
   *How:* put light, quick, playful sections first and let a tragic one break in without a bridge; do not return to the light ones after it.
   *Where:* Jewish Songs op. 13, as a whole cycle. [Gnesin]
3. **New tunes in an old mode, not quotations.** Both cycles of Jewish songs use the mode of the Sabbath prayers (*Adonoy Molokh*); he quotes no synagogue chant but makes melodies the way the tradition makes its variants.
   *Works on:* melody.
   *How:* choose the prayer mode and write a new tune in it, the way a cantor varies a known shape, rather than borrowing a tune. Which notes the mode has is not given by the sources read (see "Not checked").
   *Where:* Jewish Songs op. 13 and op. 17. [mus.academy]
4. **Not really childish:** spiky energy and angular lines under the children's words.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *Why:* in Stalin's Russia the Jewish content of the poems could not be named, so the cycle was called children's songs; there is little that is childish in it. [MusicWeb] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* keep a children's text and a simple pulse, but give the tune angular leaps and sharp accents.
   *Where:* Children's Songs op. 13, the four light songs. [MusicWeb]
5. **A simple singing tune at the start, low and soft,** on the clarinet; the piano answers with a playful, wandering tune over a rather mechanical bass. The piano stays bare and plain all through.
   *Works on:* texture, colour.
   *How:* begin with one wind alone in its low register, softly and smoothly; answer it with a playful line on the piano over a bass that repeats like a machine; keep the accompaniment thin throughout.
   *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, first movement, the opening. [Uniarts]
6. **A dance tune over an even, plain accompaniment,** with the seconds and minor thirds of klezmer, odd trills, grace notes like sobs, and wailing lines.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *Why:* a memory of carefree Jewish family life in Poland before the war, seen as a child sees it. [Uniarts] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* over a plain accompaniment in even notes, a dance tune moving by seconds and minor thirds, with grace notes before its strong notes, trills in odd places, and lines that slide and wail.
   *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, second movement (*Allegretto*). [Uniarts]
7. **The folk manner is pushed until it turns:** the grace notes and trills come more and more often, the half-step figures insist, and at the height what was carefree is desperate.
   *Works on:* melody, form.
   *Why:* like an unwanted memory that keeps breaking into one's thoughts, until the carefree turns to sorrow and despair. [Uniarts] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* repeat the dance with more ornaments each time and with its half-step figures said more and more often, louder, until the same material sounds desperate.
   *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, second movement, up to its climax. [Uniarts]
8. **The stuck record:** at that height the piano repeats one fragment so stubbornly that it sounds like a machine.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *Why:* the pianist seems to have gone, and the part sounds like a piano roll or a record that has stuck: the memory that will not leave. [Uniarts] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* at the height, take one short fragment of the accompaniment and repeat it unchanged, many more times than is natural.
   *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, second movement, the climax. [Uniarts]
9. **Then it calms, the opening comes back soft, and the last chord is major.** All three movements of the sonata end on a major chord, two of them after sorrow.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *How:* after the height, slow down, bring back the opening softly, and end on a major chord even when the music before it was in the minor and sad.
   *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, the end of each movement. [Uniarts]
10. **A solo that plays alone:** the slow movement opens with the piano alone, then the clarinet alone, broken only by a few deep chords. He also wrote twenty-four preludes for a cello alone.
    *Works on:* texture, colour.
    *Why:* the two solos carry the deepest sorrow of the sonata (David Fanning's reading, as the source reports it). [Uniarts] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* let each player have a long passage alone, the other answering only with a few low chords; at the end, let the solo line come back to its opening tune and fade.
    *Where:* Clarinet Sonata op. 28, third movement (*Adagio*); the 24 Preludes for cello. [Uniarts; Wikipedia]
11. **Clarinet and violin** are the solo voices he favours in orchestral works; laments in the slow music, klezmer in the lively.
    *Works on:* colour.
    *How:* in an orchestral piece, give the solos to a clarinet or a violin: a lament when the music is slow, a klezmer manner when it is lively.
    *Where:* the orchestral works; the finale of the Fourth Symphony for the klezmer manner. [BBC Music Magazine]
12. **Clear home notes:** his music mostly has strongly marked tonal centres; harsher from the late 1960s.
    *Works on:* harmony.
    *How:* keep a clear keynote even where the harmony is sharp; save harsher sounds for later, darker pieces.
    *Where:* most of his music; the Tenth Symphony, for strings, for the harsher later manner. [BBC Music Magazine]
13. **Plucked strings:** his Piano Quintet lost a prize because a juror objected to its plucking.
    *Works on:* colour.
    *How:* use plucked strings as a colour of their own, for whole passages.
    *Where:* the Piano Quintet. [Wikipedia]
14. **Music for children and for the circus and cartoons:** a ballet from a children's show, the songs of the Soviet *Winnie-the-Pooh*, much circus music; lullabies with a rocking motion all through (op. 110).
    *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
    *Why:* his lullabies keep rocking, but the poems carry an adult's need for comfort and a comment on society, so they do not stay simple. [MusicWeb] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* for a lullaby, keep one rocking figure going from first bar to last; for children's music, short tunes, clear rhythms and bright colours.
    *Where:* *Rocking the Child* op. 110; *Winnie-the-Pooh* (1969); the ballet *The Golden Key*. [Wikipedia; MusicWeb]

### The string quartets (from the Sixth, 1946)

He wrote seventeen string quartets. The Sixth (1946), over half an hour long, is now counted a masterpiece comparable with Shostakovich's quartets; after it he wrote no quartet for eleven years [Darwin].

15. **Violent pizzicato chords as accompaniment.** In a tender, ethereal slow movement of the Sixth, the cello sings in long notes over a frantic accompaniment, and each of its gestures ends on a violent *sforzando* pizzicato chord in the viola; pizzicato chords of this kind accompany throughout his quartets from the Third on.
    *Works on:* texture, colour, loudness.
    *Why:* the plucked chord brings a hint of violence into music that is otherwise tender. [Darwin, quoting Elphick] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* under a slow, singing line, end each phrase on a sudden, loud plucked chord in one of the accompanying instruments.
    *Where:* String Quartet no. 6, a slow movement (the source does not give the bars); his quartets from the Third. [Darwin, quoting Elphick]
16. **Tunes in thirds across the instruments:** a melody harmonised a third apart by two instruments.
    *Works on:* melody, texture.
    *How:* give the tune to two instruments together, a third apart.
    *Where:* his quartets from the Third; the Sixth's last movement. [Darwin, quoting Elphick]
17. **Chromatic slips.** A melody slips by a half step off its scale for a moment.
    *Works on:* melody.
    *Why:* the slip gives the tune a suggestion of cheekiness. [Darwin, quoting Elphick] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* now and then, move a note of the tune a half step off the scale and back, or carry on from it.
    *Where:* his quartets from the Third. [Darwin, quoting Elphick]
18. **Flattened modes.** He uses modes and not only major and minor: in the Sixth the Locrian, the mode whose second and fifth are flattened, is a main focus of the last movement.
    *Works on:* harmony, melody.
    *How:* lower the second, and at times the fifth, of the scale, so that the keynote sounds unsteady.
    *Where:* String Quartet no. 6, first and last movements. [Darwin; Darwin, quoting Elphick]
19. **Melancholy and flurries:** the Sixth opens on a melancholy theme in slow half notes set against rapid flurries; in the development the same material returns much more aggressively.
    *Works on:* rhythm, form.
    *How:* set a slow, sad theme in long notes against sudden runs; bring it back later hard and loud.
    *Where:* String Quartet no. 6, first movement. [Darwin]
20. **A slow fugato, the instruments entering obediently in turn,** after a violent movement; and a last movement that alternates major and minor, after Schubert, and recalls the earlier movements before a gentle close.
    *Works on:* form, texture.
    *How:* after violence, let the instruments enter one by one with the same slow subject; at the end, alternate the major and the minor and bring back earlier themes, softly.
    *Where:* String Quartet no. 6, fourth, fifth and last movements. [Darwin; Darwin, quoting Elphick]
21. **The destruction of childhood.** Many of his works draw their images from his memories of childhood and of the Second World War; themes of the destruction of childhood, and so of purity, serenity and stability, are central to his aesthetic and often symbolised by the musical material; against them he strove for a universal harmony through neoclassical clarity and proportion.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: what his music is about, as the source puts it.
    *Where:* his works in general. [Darwin, quoting Nikitina in Grove]

## Numbers

- **Clarinet Sonata op. 28**: about twenty minutes, three movements: *Allegro*, *Allegretto*, *Adagio*; each ends on a major chord. [Uniarts]
- **Children's Songs op. 13** (1943, poems by Peretz): a wordless introduction, four light songs, a song of grief, and a coda on the introduction's material, sadder. [MusicWeb; Oxford Song]

## What he does not do

*By these sources.*

- Quote folk or synagogue tunes: he writes his own in their manner.
- Fill the accompaniment: it stays bare.
- End in the minor after a sorrowful movement (in the clarinet sonata).

## Too well known to borrow

- **The songs of the Soviet *Winnie-the-Pooh*** (1969): his best-known cartoon score, whose songs entered popular culture in Russia. No other figure of his is called famous by the sources read. [Wikipedia]
