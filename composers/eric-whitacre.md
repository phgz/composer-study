# Eric Whitacre (born 1970)

Begun by Claude on 23 November 2026, from the sources at the end, read that day. It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** Two kinds of piece: his music for wind band, *October* (2000) and the band version of *Lux Aurumque* (2005); and how the chords of his unaccompanied choral works are made, with *Water Night* (1995) walked through (added on 24 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 71). **Scores:** none in score-reader's catalogue (he is living; all in copyright), so nothing below was checked against his notes.

## Techniques

1. **A wind sings alone at the start, over almost nothing.** *October* opens *pianissimo*: one held note in the first clarinets, wind chimes, and a solo oboe with the opening melody. In the band *Lux Aurumque* an oboe takes the soprano's solo.
   *Works on:* texture, form, colour.
   *How:* begin very softly with one held note in the clarinets and a light shimmer; let one wind (an oboe) sing the first tune alone over it.
   *Where:* *October*, the opening; *Lux Aurumque* for band, the oboe's solo. [Wikipedia, October; Milne]
2. **Chords of neighbouring notes that thicken and thin.** Much of his music uses clusters drawn from the notes of the key, which grow or thin in density by dividing the parts (up to 18 in the choral works).
   *Works on:* harmony, texture.
   *How:* build chords from notes of the key that sit next to one another (a second apart); make a chord grow by dividing a section into more parts, each taking another neighbouring note, and thin it the same way.
   *Where:* across his works; in the band *Lux Aurumque* through its thirteen colourings. [Milne, citing Shrock]
3. **Chords built in seconds, fourths and fifths,** more than in thirds.
   *Works on:* harmony.
   *Why:* in the climax, fourths in the bass add to the tension and release that the piece is built on. [Milne] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* space chords with seconds, fourths and fifths between their notes, and use the third as one colour among them rather than as the frame.
   *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*. [Milne]
4. **The keynote is avoided for long stretches, and the dominant is softened** by suspensions and added notes. In *Lux Aurumque*: a triad on the sixth degree moves to a chord on the seventh with its fourth suspended, twice, where a cadence would be.
   *Works on:* harmony.
   *Why:* the suspensions (bars 30 to 35 of the band *Lux Aurumque*) give each line room to be heard inside the chord. [Milne] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* where a cadence is expected, go from the chord on the sixth degree to a chord on the seventh with a suspended fourth, instead of to the keynote; keep the bass off the keynote for long stretches and add notes to the dominant.
   *Where:* *Lux Aurumque*, twice where a cadence would be (the source does not give the bars). [Milne]
5. **One idea, in changing colours.** He calls it the "golden brick": a single idea set against a wide palette. The band *Lux Aurumque* gives its one material thirteen different instrumental colourings where the choral version has three voicings.
   *Works on:* form, colour.
   *Why:* he says that holding to one idea is what gives a piece unity, direction and purpose. [Milne] *(his words)*
   *How:* keep one short idea throughout, and change its scoring at each return: other instruments, other registers, other doublings.
   *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*: one material, thirteen colourings. [Milne]
6. **The clarinets are the body of the band.** They are in all thirteen colourings; the brass alone never plays.
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* the clarinets and saxophones are chosen for how much they can do; horns with trombones are used where the band's mixed sound has to blend. [Milne] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* let the clarinets play in every passage as the core of the sound; add other winds and brass to them, never brass alone.
   *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*. [Milne]
7. **Low instruments held back.** Bass clarinet and baritone saxophone wait a bar; euphonium and tuba enter at bar 14; the contrabassoon enters only at the climax, two octaves below the line it doubles.
   *Works on:* form, colour.
   *Why:* at the opening the low instruments would spoil the balance and clarity of each two-note chord; held out, they keep their low sound for the word *gravisque* (heavy), where they come in; the contrabassoon is added for its own colour and range. [Milne] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* bring the lowest instruments in late and one by one; save the very lowest for the climax, doubling a line two octaves down.
   *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*: bass clarinet and baritone saxophone from bar 2, euphonium and tuba from bar 14, the contrabassoon at the climax. [Milne]
8. **The climax is made by doubling, octaves and suspensions,** with a horn call on top.
   *Works on:* texture, loudness.
   *Why:* the doubling, the octaves and the suspensions are there to make the climax stronger; he wanted the horns to "sing high and triumphant". [Milne] *(an analysis; his words)*
   *How:* make the loud place by doubling the tune in octaves through the band, holding notes over into the next chord, and putting a horn call above.
   *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*, its climax. [Milne]
9. **One true climax, near the end.** *October* has five places where the full band is loud but only one real climax, just before the end.
   *Works on:* form, loudness.
   *How:* let the full band be loud several times, but keep one place louder and fuller than all the others, just before the end.
   *Where:* *October*, just before the end. [Wikipedia, October]
10. **Slow.** The band *Lux Aurumque* is marked *Adagio, molto legato*, a quarter at 56, slower than the choral version.
    *Works on:* rhythm.
    *Why:* perhaps to give the sound more time to die away; the thesis offers this as likely, not as known. [Milne] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* very slow (a quarter around 56) and joined, so that each chord has time to blend and die away.
    *Where:* the band *Lux Aurumque*. [Milne]
11. **It ends by fading.** *October* closes in a long diminuendo into silence; in *Lux Aurumque* the first clarinet holds one note from bar 41 to the end.
    *Works on:* form, loudness.
    *Why:* in *Lux Aurumque* the clarinet perhaps holds the last note for its colour and because its breath is easy to control (the thesis's guess). [Milne] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* end with a long fade; or let one clarinet hold a single note through the last bars while the rest drop away.
    *Where:* *October*, the coda; the band *Lux Aurumque*, bar 41 to the end. [Wikipedia, October; Milne]
12. ***October*'s layout:** an introduction, four themes and a coda, 113 bars; a solo euphonium over clarinet tremolo begins the third theme; the oboe comes back in an interlude. Keys by signature: D flat, A flat, B flat, G. Bars of 2/4, 4/4 and 6/4. Eighth notes rule the first half, quarters and halves the second.
    *Works on:* form.
    *Why:* the order small group, full band, small group is what gives the serene, expressive mood he aimed at; the euphonium's solo tells by its contrast with the clarinets' trills. [Wikipedia, October] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* a model for a band piece of about five minutes: an introduction, four tunes in turn with a key for each, an interlude that brings back the first solo instrument, a coda; quicker notes in the first half, longer ones in the second.
    *Where:* *October*, as a whole. [Wikipedia, October]
13. **What he said of *October*:** simple pastoral melodies and harmonies after the English Romantics (Vaughan Williams, Elgar); and that not enough lush, beautiful music is written for winds.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: it says what he aimed at, simple pastoral tunes and harmony in the English manner, written for winds.
    *Where:* his note on *October*, as reported. [Wikipedia, October]

### The choral works: how the chords are made

*Water Night* (1995) is for choir without accompaniment, its sections divided into three, four and five parts; it was commissioned by the Dale Warland Singers [Wikipedia, Water Night].

14. **Simple lines, dense chords.** However rich the harmony, each voice's line is simple, mostly steps and the notes of a triad; the dense chords come from where these simple lines meet and cross, with the sections divided into many parts (five notes or more in a choir of four).
    *Works on:* harmony, texture, melody.
    *Why:* it is the crossing of simple parts that makes the dense harmony, and the process can take precedence over traditional harmonic function and voice leading, or replace them. [Larson] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* give each part a plain line by step or along a chord; place the lines so that, together, they make chords of five or more notes; divide a section to add a part.
    *Where:* his choral music in general (the excerpt names no work). [Larson]
15. **Notes gathering and falling away.** Neighbouring chords hold different numbers of notes: in some passages notes accrue from chord to chord, in others they disappear, and he moves freely between thin and dense.
    *Works on:* harmony, texture.
    *How:* from one chord to the next, add a note, then another, without taking any away; elsewhere take them away one at a time.
    *Where:* his choral music in general. [Larson]
16. **A tune that leaves its notes behind.** A melody climbs or falls through the notes of the chord it means, and each of its notes is kept by at least one voice after it sounds, so that the chord is built from the tune.
    *Works on:* melody, harmony, texture.
    *How:* write the tune through the notes of the chord to come; as the tune moves on, let another part take each of its notes and hold it, until the tune has built the whole chord.
    *Where:* *Water Night*, bars 46 to 49. [Hall]
17. **Added notes as the rule.** By one estimate at least three chords in four are chords with added notes, not plain major or minor triads.
    *Works on:* harmony.
    *Why:* the added notes make movement where there might have been none (*With a Lily in Your Hand*, bars 1 to 3); a writer cited by the same thesis says that added seconds thicken the chord and give it momentum. [Hall] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* add a second, a fourth or a sixth to most chords; keep the plain triad rare.
    *Where:* his choral music; *With a Lily in Your Hand*, bars 1 to 3. [Hall]
18. **The keynote held while the dissonance moves over it.** *Water Night* begins on a plain B flat minor chord, its third hovering in the sopranos, and a slow succession of ninth and eleventh chords moves over that keynote chord (bars 1 to 5); the same goes on an octave lower (bars 6 to 15).
    *Works on:* harmony, texture.
    *Why:* the keynote under moving dissonance gives the opening its first impression of darkness and irregularity. [Wikipedia, Water Night] *(a listener)*
    *How:* hold the minor keynote chord low; above it, move slowly through chords with ninths and elevenths, the keynote never leaving.
    *Where:* *Water Night*, bars 1 to 15. [Wikipedia, Water Night]
19. **Call and answer, falling.** A sudden *forte*: the women on the keynote chord, the men two beats later; the call and answer, keynote under dissonance, moves down in key.
    *Works on:* texture, form, loudness.
    *How:* let the high voices call a chord loudly and the low ones answer two beats later; repeat, each time in a lower key.
    *Where:* *Water Night*, from bar 15. [Wikipedia, Water Night]
20. **One climax chord, every note filled.** The only climax is one chord, on the word "eyes": divided sopranos, altos and tenors sing a cluster of every note of the scale from A flat 5 down to E flat 4, over a G flat major chord in the basses, the sixth degree of the key. It comes inside a long crescendo (bars 28 to 52, as the article gives them). At bars 47 and 48 the gap between the basses (E flat 3) and the sopranos (D flat 4) is filled in gradually, note by note. The texture is made denser towards the climax.
    *Works on:* harmony, loudness, form.
    *How:* aim the piece at one chord: fill every scale step between two notes about an octave and a half apart, over the major chord of the sixth degree in the bass; reach it by a long crescendo and by filling the space between the outer voices.
    *Where:* *Water Night*: the climax on "eyes"; bars 28 to 52 and 47 and 48. [Wikipedia, Water Night; Hall, citing Larson]
21. **It ends where it began.** After the climax *Water Night* deflates; at the end it falls back to the opening's play of keynote under dissonance, and the last chord to ring is the B flat minor chord. *Sleep* ends with one bar written out six times over; *Cloudburst* and *A Boy and a Girl* end on the chord with which they work or begin.
    *Works on:* form, harmony.
    *How:* end with the opening's sound: the keynote chord of the minor, with the dissonance above it thinning out; or repeat one bar several times as written.
    *Where:* *Water Night*, the end; *Sleep*, its last motive; *Cloudburst*, bars 81, 83 and 84. [Wikipedia, Water Night; Hall]
22. **Slow, together, steady.** Slow speeds, all the voices moving together, a melody that is hard to tell apart, a steady rhythm.
    *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
    *Why:* in *Sleep*, the effect is of a haze of sound without any sense of forward motion. [Hall, citing Lynch] *(a listener)*
    *How:* slow, in chords, the tune inside the chords rather than above them, without quick notes.
    *Where:* his choral music; *Sleep*. [Hall, citing Owen and Lynch]

## Walks through whole works

***Water Night*** (1995), as the article walks it: B flat minor, the keynote chord held under moving ninths and elevenths (bars 1 to 5), then an octave lower (6 to 15); a sudden *forte*, women and men in call and answer, moving down in key (from 15); the opening echoed (25 and 26); a duet of sopranos and altos in minor seconds and fourths, with no melody (26 and 27); a long crescendo (28 to 52) with the one climax chord on "eyes"; a deflation to bar 41; the piece's most notable melodic line (42); the gap between basses and sopranos filled in (47 and 48); the end falls back to the opening, the B flat minor chord ringing last. [Wikipedia, Water Night]

## Numbers

- ***October*** (2000): 113 bars; an introduction, four themes and a coda; bars of 2/4, 4/4 and 6/4; five places where the full band is loud and one real climax, just before the end (techniques 9 and 12). [Wikipedia, October]
- **The band *Lux Aurumque*** (2005): *Adagio, molto legato*, a quarter at 56 (technique 10). [Milne]

## What he does not do

- Let the brass play alone (*Lux Aurumque* for band).
- Close on a plain cadence where he has set one up.
- End loud.

## Too well known to borrow

- ***Lux Aurumque*** for choir: his most famous work, sung by choirs everywhere, at Christmas above all; also *Sleep* and *The Seal Lullaby*. [BBC Music Magazine, Whitacre]
- **The sound itself:** "sustained crushed harmonies" are called his hallmark. A choir holding close clusters is heard as Whitacre at once, which is one reason the film sound is near (techniques 2 and 3). [BBC Music Magazine, Whitacre]
