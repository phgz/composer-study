# Eric Whitacre (born 1970)

Begun by Claude on 23 November 2026, from the sources at the end, read that day. It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** One kind of piece: his music for wind band, *October* (2000) and the band version of *Lux Aurumque* (2005). **Scores:** none in score-reader's catalogue (he is living; all in copyright), so nothing below was checked against his notes. ## Techniques

1. **A wind sings alone at the start, over almost nothing.** *October* opens *pianissimo*: one held note in the first clarinets, wind chimes, and a solo oboe with the opening melody. In the band *Lux Aurumque* an oboe takes the soprano's solo. [Wikipedia, October; Milne]
2. **Chords of neighbouring notes that thicken and thin.** Much of his music uses clusters drawn from the notes of the key, which grow or thin in density by dividing the parts (up to 18 in the choral works). [Milne, citing Shrock]
3. **Chords built in seconds, fourths and fifths,** more than in thirds. [Milne]
4. **The keynote is avoided for long stretches, and the dominant is softened** by suspensions and added notes. In *Lux Aurumque*: a triad on the sixth degree moves to a chord on the seventh with its fourth suspended, twice, where a cadence would be. [Milne]
5. **One idea, in changing colours.** He calls it the "golden brick": a single idea set against a wide palette. The band *Lux Aurumque* gives its one material thirteen different instrumental colourings where the choral version has three voicings. [Milne]
6. **The clarinets are the body of the band.** They are in all thirteen colourings; the brass alone never plays. [Milne]
7. **Low instruments held back.** Bass clarinet and baritone saxophone wait a bar; euphonium and tuba enter at bar 14; the contrabassoon enters only at the climax, two octaves below the line it doubles. [Milne]
8. **The climax is made by doubling, octaves and suspensions,** with a horn call on top. [Milne]
9. **One true climax, near the end.** *October* has five places where the full band is loud but only one real climax, just before the end. [Wikipedia, October]
10. **Slow.** The band *Lux Aurumque* is marked *Adagio, molto legato*, a quarter at 56, slower than the choral version so that the sound can blend and decay. [Milne]
11. **It ends by fading.** *October* closes in a long diminuendo into silence; in *Lux Aurumque* the first clarinet holds one note from bar 41 to the end. [Wikipedia, October; Milne]
12. ***October*'s layout:** an introduction, four themes and a coda, 113 bars; a solo euphonium over clarinet tremolo begins the third theme; the oboe comes back in an interlude. Keys by signature: D flat, A flat, B flat, G. Bars of 2/4, 4/4 and 6/4. Eighth notes rule the first half, quarters and halves the second. [Wikipedia, October]
13. **What he said of *October*:** simple pastoral melodies and harmonies after the English Romantics (Vaughan Williams, Elgar); and that not enough lush, beautiful music is written for winds. [Wikipedia, October]

## Numbers

- ***October*** (2000): 113 bars; an introduction, four themes and a coda; bars of 2/4, 4/4 and 6/4; five places where the full band is loud and one real climax, just before the end (techniques 9 and 12). [Wikipedia, October]
- **The band *Lux Aurumque*** (2005): *Adagio, molto legato*, a quarter at 56 (technique 10). [Milne]

## What he does not do

- Let the brass play alone (*Lux Aurumque* for band).
- Close on a plain cadence where he has set one up.
- End loud.
