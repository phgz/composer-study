# Julia Wolfe (born 1958)

Begun by Claude on 25 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 74, from the sources at the end, read on 24 and 25 November 2026. It grows each time she is used.

**Scope so far.** One kind of piece: her oratorios on American folklore and labour for voices and the amplified Bang on a Can ensemble, *Steel Hammer* (2009) and *Anthracite Fields* (2014); with one line on the string quartet *Four Marys* (1991). **Scores:** none in score-reader's catalogue (she is living; all in copyright), so nothing below was checked against notes. It rests on programme notes (two with her own words), an encyclopaedia and two reviews.

**Measured profile:** none: no work of hers is in score-reader's catalogue.

## Techniques

### The folk and labour oratorios (*Steel Hammer*, *Anthracite Fields*)

1. **Many versions of one ballad, broken and woven into one.** For *Steel Hammer* she drew on the extreme variations of the John Henry story (over 200 versions, circulating since the late 1800s), fragmenting and weaving the contradictory versions into a new whole, and sometimes meditating on single words or phrases.
   *Works on:* form, melody.
   *Why:* to tell "the story of the story", and to embody the many paths it travelled at once. [Cal Performances, Steel Hammer] *(her words)*
   *How:* take a tune and write several versions of it that disagree (another note, another rhythm, another ending); break each into its phrases; build the piece from those fragments, sometimes stopping on one phrase and repeating it at length.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*, throughout. [Cal Performances, Steel Hammer; Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
2. **Words repeated and broken down, as on an early tape loop.** The opening movement repeats its text and breaks it down in a way the encyclopaedia compares to Steve Reich's early tape pieces.
   *Works on:* rhythm, melody.
   *How:* repeat a short phrase many times and take it apart, losing a piece of it, or shifting it, each time round.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*, "Some Say". [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
3. **Oscillating minor thirds and cycling syncopations.** The score draws on minimalist techniques, oscillating minor thirds and cycling, syncopated rhythms, and a language beyond them.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
   *Why:* a radio critic heard her music there as "totally relentless, like a steam-powered drill" boring through a mountain. [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer] *(a listener)*
   *How:* a figure that rocks between two notes a minor third apart, in a syncopated rhythm that comes round again and again, not always at the bar line.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*. [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
4. **Dark dissonances close to cacophony.**
   *Works on:* harmony, loudness.
   *How:* where the story turns hard, pile up clashing notes, loud, until they are near noise.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*, "Destiny". [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
5. **A rhythm stamped out by a player.** "Characteristics" is built on a flamenco-like rhythm that a player stamps out.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* build a movement on one rhythm, struck by the body (feet, hands), under everything else.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*, "Characteristics". [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
6. **Folk instruments beside the amplified ensemble.** Banjo and mountain dulcimer lines give a bluegrass sound; gentle electric guitar figures add a hint of spookiness; the 2021 production added mountain dulcimer, wooden bones, banjo, harmonicas and body percussion to the usual sextet (clarinets, piano, percussion, electric guitar, cello, bass).
   *Works on:* colour.
   *How:* beside the clarinet, piano, cello, bass and percussion, give a plucked folk instrument a line of its own, and an electric guitar soft figures.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*. [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer; Cal Performances, Steel Hammer]
7. **Voices that for a moment sound like plainchant.**
   *Works on:* melody.
   *How:* at the close, let the voices sing a slow line, in unison or close, without pulse.
   *Where:* *Steel Hammer*, "Lord Lord", the last movement, which is about the hammer. [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer; Cal Performances, Steel Hammer]
8. **A list of names, sung in an order.** "Foundation" sets the names of miners from an index of accidents (1869 to 1916): only the Johns with one-syllable surnames, in alphabetical order, the movement ending with names of several syllables; geological descriptions of how coal forms sit at its centre. Men chant the names.
   *Works on:* form, rhythm.
   *Why:* she meant to sing every victim's name and was alarmed by how many there were; the diversity of the miners' origins is heard in the names. [Cal Performances, Anthracite Fields; Wikipedia, Anthracite Fields] *(her words)*
   *How:* choose a list with a rule (one kind of name, one order), set it as a chant on a few notes, and let the list's own sounds make the rhythm.
   *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Foundation". [Cal Performances, Anthracite Fields; Gramophone]
9. **Percussion that imitates the work.** In "Breaker Boys" a cowbell and bicycle pedals imitate falling coal; the rhythm gives the movement a rock feel.
   *Works on:* colour, rhythm.
   *How:* find objects whose sound is the sound of the work being sung about, and make them the rhythm section.
   *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Breaker Boys". [Wikipedia, Anthracite Fields]
10. **Few textures for a long span.** The twenty-minute "Foundation" is built on just five primary harmonic and rhythmic textures: rumbling piano and electric guitar, gritty cello and bass, a gently wailing bass clarinet; the granitic texture softens gradually as the momentum builds, and the voices blend with it and emerge from it.
    *Works on:* form, texture.
    *How:* choose a handful of textures and stay in each a long time; let the voices come out of the instruments' sound rather than over it.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Foundation". [Arts Fuse]
11. **Repetition to the limit.** The reviewer finds she enjoys pushing the limits of how many times she can repeat a gesture or a theme.
    *Works on:* form.
    *How:* repeat a gesture past the point where a listener expects it to stop.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*. [Arts Fuse]
12. **One let-up, then a relentless build to the end.** "Breaker Boys" drives furiously on, its energy letting up only once, in the middle; its last half builds relentlessly over the thunder of a drum kit. "Speech" and "Appliances" also build to driving climaxes.
    *Works on:* form, loudness.
    *How:* keep the drive from the start; stop it once, near the middle; then build without a break to the end.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Breaker Boys", "Speech", "Appliances". [Arts Fuse]
13. **A list of flowers, folk-like, as a rest.** "Flowers" comes from a miner's daughter recalling that every family kept a garden: a long sung list of flower names, simple and folk-like, the singers prominently accompanied by an acoustic guitar, with repetition at the end; a break in the action.
    *Works on:* melody, form.
    *How:* between driven movements, a plain song over a guitar, its words a list, its last phrase repeated.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Flowers". [Cal Performances, Anthracite Fields; Arts Fuse; Gramophone]
14. **A solo manifesto against thick ensemble responses.**
    *Works on:* texture.
    *How:* one voice speaks or sings a declaration; after each sentence the whole ensemble answers, thick.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*, "Speech" (from a speech by John L. Lewis, of the mine workers' union). [Gramophone]
15. **Interlocking piano pulses and an industrial thrum.** The piano's rhythms pulsate and interlock; the electric guitar provides an industrial thrum; the drums a deliberately grungy beat. In "Appliances" the instruments flicker like a faulty light bulb.
    *Works on:* rhythm, colour.
    *How:* two piano parts in short repeated patterns that fit into each other; a guitar holding one low, buzzing sound; short broken figures that stop and start.
    *Where:* *Anthracite Fields*. [Gramophone]

### The string quartet (*Four Marys*, 1991)

16. **The mountain dulcimer magnified in a string quartet.** *Four Marys* takes the sound world of the mountain dulcimer and magnifies it: the sliding pitches, the crude crying tone, the drone strings and the "strumming" expand through the quartet. She wrote it for a quartet that played "as if they were one organism".
    *Works on:* colour, texture.
    *How:* give the strings drones on open strings, slides between notes, a rough crying tone, and chords strummed across the strings.
    *Where:* *Four Marys*. [Wolfe, Four Marys] *(her words)*

## Walks through whole works

***Steel Hammer*** (79 minutes; nine movements, from the programme and the encyclopaedia, which give the order and a line on each, not bars). "Some Say": the sources disagree about John Henry's origins, the text repeated and broken down (2); "The States": the places the versions name; "Destiny": his childhood and his vow, dark dissonances near cacophony (4); "Mountain": the mountain against his smallness; "Characteristics": contradictory descriptions of him, over a stamped rhythm (5); "Polly Ann": his wife, under many names, who drives steel when he falls ill; "The Race": against the steam drill; "Winner": the drill's inventor boasts, but John Henry drives fourteen feet to the drill's nine; "Lord Lord": the hammer, voices briefly like plainchant (7). [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer; Cal Performances, Steel Hammer]

***Anthracite Fields*** (about an hour; five movements). "Foundation" (about twenty minutes): the names (8), five textures (10), geology at its centre; "Breaker Boys": a schoolyard rhyme, falling coal in the percussion (9), driving, one let-up (12); "Speech" (14); "Flowers" (13), the rest; "Appliances": everyday uses of power, flickering instruments (15), ending on a 1900 railway advertisement. [Cal Performances, Anthracite Fields; Wikipedia, Anthracite Fields; Arts Fuse; Gramophone]

## Numbers

- ***Steel Hammer*** (2009): three sopranos (Trio Mediaeval at the first performance) and the Bang on a Can All-Stars (clarinet, percussion, guitar, piano, cello, bass); 79 minutes; nine movements. [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]
- ***Anthracite Fields*** (2014): chorus and the six-player amplified ensemble (cello, bass, piano and keyboard, percussion, guitar, clarinet and bass clarinet); about an hour; five movements; Pulitzer Prize for Music, 2015. [Wikipedia, Anthracite Fields; Cal Performances, Anthracite Fields]
- ***Four Marys*** (1991): string quartet, 11 minutes. [Wolfe, Four Marys]

## What she does not do

*In the oratorios.*

- **Not a narrative:** *Anthracite Fields* looks at its subject from different angles rather than telling a story (her words) [Wikipedia, Anthracite Fields].

## Too well known to borrow

- **The John Henry ballad** as sung (its tune and words): a folk song she drew on, not hers, and widely known (over 200 versions) [Wikipedia, Steel Hammer]. A piece takes her way of breaking a ballad, not the ballad.
