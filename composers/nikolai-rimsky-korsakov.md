## Techniques

### The orchestra, in his own words (*Principles of Orchestration*)

1. **The strings carry the melody.** The violins on top, the second violins joining the first; the cellos for a tense, passionate line, best on their A string; the violas, slightly nasal, for short characteristic phrases and usually doubled; the double basses with the cellos or the bassoons.
   *Works on:* colour, melody.
   *Why:* the strings join notes warmly and hold them, which makes them "far and away" the best melodic medium; each string has its own colour, the violin's top string brilliant. [Principles, ch. I-II] *(his words)*
   *How:* give a singing tune to the violins, or to the cellos when it should be passionate and lie high; double a viola line; never leave the double basses alone on a line.
   *Where:* his book, chapters I and II, with examples from his own works. [Principles]

2. **A tune set apart.** By colour, by doubling or by crossing above the other parts, and only where needed by accents; a very high violin tune doubled an octave below, a very low one an octave above.
   *Works on:* melody, texture.
   *Why:* a tune in the middle of the texture is lost without one of these; very high up, alone, it becomes "too isolated" and needs the octave for fullness and firmness. [Principles, ch. II] *(his words)*
   *How:* before writing an accompaniment, decide how the tune will be heard over it: another colour, a doubling, or a higher place; double a very high tune at the octave below.
   *Where:* his book, chapter II. [Principles]

3. **Woodwinds in their natural order, and the pairs that work.** Flutes on top, then oboes, clarinets, bassoons; flute with oboe in unison is fuller than the flute and sweeter than the oboe; oboe with clarinet is fuller than either; flute and bassoon two octaves apart do not work.
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* each woodwind has a character (the flute cold, the oboe artless, the clarinet pliable); an unnatural order is "far-fetched"; registers too far apart do not join. [Principles, ch. I-II] *(his words)*
   *How:* stack woodwinds by their order; for a fuller single line, put two different woodwinds in unison; save the strange orders for a strange effect.
   *Where:* his book, chapters I and II. [Principles]

4. **Brass for fanfares and plain tunes; horns for the soft and poetic.** Triumphant in the major, gloomy in the minor; never a passionate phrase.
   *Works on:* colour, melody.
   *Why:* the brass has power, simplicity and eloquence but no passion; a passionate phrase on it turns "sickly and insipid". [Principles, ch. II] *(his words)*
   *How:* give the brass fanfares and diatonic themes; give a soft, tender phrase to the horns; keep the passionate line in the strings.
   *Where:* his book, chapter II. [Principles]

5. **Strings with brass: which pairs join.** Strings and brass side by side are each heard too distinctly; violin with trumpet, viola with horn, cellos with horns join best; woodwinds over brass in unison soften it.
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* their colours are too unlike to blend; those pairs are the closest. [Principles, ch. I-II] *(his words)*
   *How:* to double a string line in the brass, choose the pair; to soften a brass tune, put a woodwind on it.
   *Where:* his book, chapters I and II. [Principles]

6. **Harmony settled before it is scored, and scored by colour.** Wide intervals low, close ones high; gaps filled in a loud chord; the number of parts changes only where a new idea begins; in the woodwinds the concords to one colour and the dissonances to another, the held notes to one colour and the moving ones to another.
   *Works on:* harmony, colour, texture.
   *Why:* a fault in the movement of the parts spoils the sound whatever the instruments; giving a dissonance another colour, or a held note another colour than a moving one, keeps each heard. [Principles, ch. III] *(his words)*
   *How:* write the chords in four real parts first; then give each held note and each moving line its own colour, and lay out a chord wide at the bottom and close at the top.
   *Where:* his book, chapter III. [Principles]

7. **A swell made by adding groups.** Strings, then woodwinds, then brass for a crescendo; the reverse for a diminuendo; the whole orchestra only for a while.
   *Works on:* loudness, colour.
   *Why:* "neither musical feeling nor the ear" can bear the full orchestra for long. [Principles, ch. IV] *(his words)*
   *How:* grow a passage by bringing in the groups in that order, each with the same music; thin it in the reverse order; keep the full tutti short.
   *Where:* his book, chapter IV. [Principles]

8. **A phrase passed between instruments.** The last note of one instrument's part is the first note of the next.
   *Works on:* texture, colour, melody.
   *Why:* for a phrase wider than one instrument's range, or to divide a phrase between two colours. [Principles, ch. IV] *(his words)*
   *How:* cut a long line where two instruments share a note, and let that note belong to both.
   *Where:* his book, chapter IV. [Principles]

9. **Echo and imitation by colour.** A phrase answered higher goes to a higher instrument; an echo is softer and close in colour; muted brass sounds far away; strings do not echo winds, nor winds strings.
   *Works on:* colour, form.
   *Why:* an echo must sound like the same thing farther off; strings and winds are too unlike for that. [Principles, ch. IV] *(his words)*
   *How:* for an echo, repeat a phrase softer on a related colour (a muted trumpet after a trumpet); for an answer higher up, choose the higher instrument.
   *Where:* his book, chapter IV. [Principles]

10. **Colour spent with care.** Strings are the ground; percussion seldom; a group kept silent gains interest when it returns; horns and trombones come back best very soft or very loud.
    *Works on:* colour, form.
    *Why:* the winds, plucked strings and percussion tire the listener sooner than the strings; a colour that has rested is heard anew. [Principles, ch. I and IV] *(his words)*
    *How:* rest each colour that is not the strings for long stretches before its important entry; bring the brass back at an extreme of loudness.
    *Where:* his book, chapters I and IV. [Principles]

11. **A voice accompanied lightly.** The strings, the most transparent group, under a solo voice; short notes rather than long held ones; the voice doubled only for single phrases.
    *Works on:* texture, colour.
    *Why:* the singer must be able to use every shade of loudness; long held notes cover a voice more than short ones; doubling it all the time replaces its own colour. [Principles, ch. V] *(his words)*
    *How:* under a solo voice, keep the orchestra to strings in short notes, add a held chord only softly, and double the voice only at its peaks.
    *Where:* his book, chapter V. [Principles]

12. **Bells, keyboards and percussion.** The piano in the orchestra can imitate bells or the guzli; the celesta is better where there is one; the glockenspiel and xylophone are bright and piercing; church bells are more stage properties than instruments; percussion of no fixed pitch is "purely ornamental".
    *Works on:* colour.
    *How:* for a bell sound, use the piano or celesta and the glockenspiel inside the orchestra, not real church bells; use the unpitched percussion as ornament only.
    *Where:* his book, chapter I. [Principles]

### The feast and the tale: the *Russian Easter Festival Overture* and *Scheherazade*

13. **Church bells made by the orchestra.** Built up in stages: the strings, bowed and plucked; the brass in short fanfares; the triangle and glockenspiel only near the end. A bell rhythm (long, short, short, long) for the full orchestra moving together.
    *Works on:* colour, rhythm, texture.
    *Why:* he was proud of this "bell-ness" and claimed to have invented it; the bells are the joyful congregation. [BSO; DuPage] *(his words; an analysis)*
    *How:* make a bell from several groups at once (plucked and short bowed strings, short brass chords, a little glockenspiel), all on one rhythm of long and short strokes; add the metal percussion last.
    *Where:* *Russian Easter Festival Overture*, the climaxes of the fast section (about 5:28 and 10:33 in the guide's recording) and the close. [BSO; DuPage]

14. **Chant in a free rhythm.** The slow opening in 5/2, the chant first in the woodwinds; the last section in 2/1 with bars of 3/1.
    *Works on:* rhythm, melody.
    *Why:* the uneven metre suggests the free rhythm of unaccompanied chant; the opening hymn avoids its third and fifth degrees, which gives it an old, modal sound. [BSO; DuPage] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* set a chant-like tune in a long, uneven bar (five half notes), in a few woodwinds, slowly and softly, leaving out the notes that would make it major or minor.
    *Where:* *Russian Easter Festival Overture*, the opening (*Lento mistico*) and the end. [BSO; DuPage; Wikipedia, Easter Overture]

15. **Solos between the chants.** A solo violin, a flute, a clarinet in free, improvised-sounding flights between statements of the chant; a solo trombone intoning the chant like a priest, the strings answering.
    *Works on:* texture, colour, form.
    *Why:* the solos are the light from the tomb at the Resurrection; the trombone and the strings are the priest and the people. [DuPage; BSO] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* between the plain statements of a slow tune, give one solo instrument a short free passage, almost a cadenza, over little or nothing; let a low brass instrument speak the tune in the rhythm of speech and the strings answer it.
    *Where:* *Russian Easter Festival Overture*, the introduction (violin about 0:45, cello 1:11, flute 1:29, trombone 1:45) and the slow middle (trombone recitative about 7:48). [DuPage]

16. **From the solemn to the pagan.** A slow, dark, sacred opening turns into a fast dance; plucked strings and bells for the merrymaking.
    *Works on:* form.
    *Why:* he wanted the feast's "legendary and heathen side": from the gloomy evening before Easter to the pagan-religious merrymaking of Easter morning. [Wikipedia, Easter Overture; DuPage] *(his words)*
    *How:* open with a slow, soft chant and its solos; then turn the same chants into a quick dance in the major, with plucked strings and bells, and end at the loudest.
    *Where:* *Russian Easter Festival Overture*, as a whole. [Wikipedia, Easter Overture; DuPage]

17. **A character as a solo violin over a harp.** A winding, tender melody for solo violin, the harp accompanying; it comes back in every movement.
    *Works on:* colour, form.
    *How:* give one character one solo instrument with a harp under it, and bring it back between the larger sections.
    *Where:* *Scheherazade*, the Scheherazade theme, first and last movements and the introductions. [Wikipedia, Scheherazade]

18. **The other character in the bass, on a whole-tone scale.** A grim bass motif that stresses four notes of a falling whole-tone scale (E, D, C, B♭).
    *Works on:* melody, harmony.
    *Why:* he used the whole-tone and octatonic scales mainly for the fantastic sections of his operas, the magical and supernatural; here the grim motif is the domineering Sultan. [Wikipedia, Rimsky-Korsakov; Wikipedia, Scheherazade] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* for a threatening or magical figure, build a motif low in the orchestra on the whole-tone scale, or on the scale of alternating whole and half steps.
    *Where:* *Scheherazade*, the opening; the scale of alternating steps was known in his circle as the "Korsakovian scale". [Wikipedia, Scheherazade; Wikipedia, Octatonic]

19. **Variations that change the accompaniment, not the tune.**
    *Works on:* texture, form.
    *How:* repeat the tune nearly unchanged and give each repeat a new accompaniment and new colours.
    *Where:* *Scheherazade*, second movement. [Wikipedia, Scheherazade]

20. **Keys a major third apart, side by side.**
    *Works on:* harmony.
    *How:* move from one key straight to the key a major third below or above (E major to C major).
    *Where:* *Scheherazade*, first movement. [Wikipedia, Scheherazade]

21. **Primary colours in the full orchestra.** In a loud tutti each group keeps its own layer: trombones, a string pattern, woodwind scales, percussion.
    *Works on:* colour, texture.
    *Why:* he brought out the "primary hues" of the instruments instead of mixing them. [Wikipedia, Rimsky-Korsakov] *(an analysis)*
    *How:* in a loud passage, give each group a different kind of material (a held brass tune, a running string figure, woodwind scales, percussion) rather than doubling everyone on one line.
    *Where:* *Scheherazade*, the last tutti. [Wikipedia, Rimsky-Korsakov]

22. **What he said orchestration is.** Part of "the very soul" of the work: "To orchestrate is to create"; technique can be taught, the imaginative side cannot; write for the orchestra that will play it, easily playable.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: it says that the colours are thought with the music, not added after it.
    *Where:* the author's preface to *Principles of Orchestration* (1891). [Principles]

## Walks through whole works

**Russian Easter Festival Overture** (about 14 minutes in the guide's recording; times are that recording's). A slow introduction (*Lento mistico*, 5/2): the chant "Let God arise" in the woodwinds; solo flights of violin, cello, flute and clarinet between its statements; a solo trombone with the strings answering (1:45); the chant low and soft in bassoon and tuba (3:20); harp glissandi to close (4:05). The fast section (*Allegro agitato*, 4:28) turns another chant into a dance and climaxes on the bell rhythm in the full orchestra (5:28). A slow middle on "Christ is risen" (6:04): violin harmonics, plucked strings and bells for the pagan feast, the trombone recitative (7:48). The fast music returns (9:01), climaxes again on the bells (10:33), gathers the earlier ideas at once (12:14), and ends on "Christ is risen" in the brass, at its loudest. [DuPage; BSO]

## Numbers

- **Balance, in his words:** in a loud passage one horn equals about two flutes, oboes, clarinets or bassoons, and one trumpet or one trombone about two horns; mark the horns one degree louder than the other brass. Soft, the woodwinds are about equal. [Principles, ch. I]
- **Harps:** three or four in unison to be heard against a loud orchestra. [Principles, ch. I]
- **Russian Easter Festival Overture:** about 14 minutes; the slow introduction about 4½ of them. [DuPage]

## What he does not do

*In his book.*
- **A passionate phrase in the brass.** [Principles, ch. II]
- **Strings echoing winds, or winds strings.** [Principles, ch. IV]
- **The full orchestra for long.** [Principles, ch. IV]

## Too well known to borrow

- ***Flight of the Bumblebee*** (*The Tale of Tsar Saltan*): probably the best-known excerpt from his operas. [Wikipedia, Rimsky-Korsakov]
