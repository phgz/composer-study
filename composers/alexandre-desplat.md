# Alexandre Desplat (born 1961)

Begun by Claude on 23 November 2026, for composer-blend's piece 68. It grows each time he is used.

**Scope so far.** One kind of piece: the film score, as two scores show it (*The Grand Budapest Hotel*, 2014, and *The Shape of Water*, 2017), with what he has said of his way of working. **Scores:** none: all his music is in copyright and none is in score-reader's catalogue. Nothing below was checked against notes; nothing of his may be reproduced. It rests on interviews, reviews and one analysis of the themes, so most of it is a listener's or a writer's account. ## Techniques

1. **Each film its own sound.** He avoids using the same instruments again; the sound should "belong to the film".
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* in his words, so that each score feels particular to its story. [Tatler] *(his words)*
   *How:* choose a small set of colours for the piece, unusual together, and keep to them from start to end.
   *Where:* his account of his scores, *The Shape of Water* among them. [Tatler]

2. **One family of woodwinds only.** In *The Shape of Water* the woodwinds are all flutes, twelve of them, with no oboes, clarinets or bassoons; strings, piano and harp; bass flutes for the dark cues.
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* to evoke water (the review); for him, "the feeling of water", its lukewarm warmth, which he ties to love. [MovieMusicUK; RTÉ] *(a listener; his words)*
   *How:* leave out all woodwinds but one family and write the whole woodwind part for it, high and low (flutes, alto and bass flutes).
   *Where:* *The Shape of Water*, throughout. [MovieMusicUK; RTÉ]

3. **A character as one instrument, on a trembling note.** Mandolins in tremolo for M. Gustave; an accordion for the Amphibious Man, often like a tango; his own whistling for Elisa.
   *Works on:* colour, melody.
   *Why:* the trembling of the mandolins suits Gustave's serenity and elegance; the whistling is Elisa's carefree moments. [NPR; Far Out; RTÉ] *(his words; a listener)*
   *How:* give each character one instrument that no one else has, and keep it for that character.
   *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*; *The Shape of Water*. [NPR; Far Out; RTÉ; MovieMusicUK]

4. **Folk instruments of an imagined country.** Balalaikas, a cimbalom and Gregorian chant for a Central Europe that never was.
   *Works on:* colour.
   *Why:* they draw on the story's setting; and he avoids obvious period music, which he calls "a trap". [Far Out; NPR] *(an analysis; his words)*
   *How:* evoke a place with a few of its instruments, mixed with the orchestra, and do not imitate its old music.
   *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*. [Far Out; NPR]

5. **A cimbalom alone, then the tune.** Gustave's theme begins with a solo cimbalom accompaniment, and the melody joins later; it is in the minor throughout.
   *Works on:* texture, colour, form.
   *Why:* the minor suits his seriousness; the theme fades from the film as he loses control. [Film Music Notes] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* start a theme with its accompaniment figure alone on a plucked or struck instrument; bring the melody in over it after a few bars (in the cue, at about 0:34).
   *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, Gustave's theme. [Film Music Notes]

6. **A major tune clouded by its flat sixth.** Zero's theme, the only major one of the score, is in C with an A flat that clouds it; when the theme comes back in a happier scene the A flat is gone.
   *Works on:* harmony, melody.
   *Why:* the flat sixth is a bittersweet longing; taking it away makes the theme plainly positive. [Film Music Notes] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a tune in the major and colour it with the minor's sixth; in a later, happier return, play it without that note.
   *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, Zero's theme. [Film Music Notes]

7. **A scale with a raised fourth and a lowered second, on the organ.** Jopling's theme.
   *Works on:* harmony, colour.
   *Why:* scored "fearfully" on organ, for a melodramatic horror effect. [Film Music Notes] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* build a menacing figure on a minor-sounding scale with a lowered second and a raised fourth, and give it to the organ, short.
   *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, Jopling's theme (the organ cue, its first seconds). [Film Music Notes]

8. **A short staccato motif that loses its danger.** The Amphibious Man's five-note staccato motif begins aggressive and gradually becomes gentle.
   *Works on:* melody, form.
   *Why:* the creature ceases to be a threat. [MovieMusicUK] *(a listener)*
   *How:* give a figure of a few short notes a hard, quick first statement; at each return make it softer, slower and warmer in colour, keeping its notes.
   *Where:* *The Shape of Water*. [MovieMusicUK]

9. **Themes varied, never only restated.** The themes come back changed in key, scoring, speed and intent; two themes are joined in a third.
   *Works on:* form, melody.
   *How:* at each return change at least one of key, instruments, speed or character; at the end make one theme out of two.
   *Where:* *The Shape of Water*. [MovieMusicUK]

10. **Fragile colours to begin, the full orchestra later.** The opening cue joins harp, waterphone, glass harmonica and piano, and widens to the full orchestra afterwards.
    *Works on:* colour, form.
    *How:* open with a few fragile, ringing sounds (harp, glass, piano high), and let the orchestra come in only later.
    *Where:* *The Shape of Water*, the opening cue. [MovieMusicUK]

11. **Suspense by pulse and low woodwinds.** Ticking or wooden percussion, lurching rhythms, rattling bass flutes, tremolo strings; cello pulses under high violins.
    *Works on:* rhythm, colour.
    *How:* for tension, a steady pulse in the low strings or wooden percussion, a theme's fragment in the bass flutes, tremolo above.
    *Where:* *The Shape of Water*, the suspense cues ("Fingers", "The Escape"). [MovieMusicUK]

12. **Not restating what is seen.** The score says what the screen cannot: the inner life of the characters, the film's themes.
    *Works on:* background.
    *Why:* in his words, "the last thing you should do is restate what is obvious". [Far Out] *(his words)*
    *How:* not a technique on its own: it says the music carries what is not shown, not what is.
    *Where:* his account of his work. [Far Out]

13. **Not too serious.** In a film that mixes a dark view of people with a distant humour, the music must not take itself too seriously.
    *Works on:* background.
    *Why:* in his words, too serious a score would undercut the humour. [NPR] *(his words)*
    *How:* not a technique on its own: keep the lightness of the colours (mandolins, cimbalom, a brushed snare) even in the sad themes.
    *Where:* *The Grand Budapest Hotel*. [NPR]

14. **Where he comes from.** A flautist from childhood (also piano and trumpet); French composers, Ravel and Debussy above all, jazz and world music; Herrmann's, Rota's, Jarre's and Delerue's film scores; rhythms of Greek, African and Brazilian music and salsa; orchestration learnt by studying scores (Mozart, Wagner).
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: where his manner comes from.
    *Where:* Wikipedia; his interview with Tatler. [Wikipedia; Tatler]

### Keyboards for a racing mind: *The Imitation Game*

15. **Five keyboards at once for the speed of a mind.** Three pianos (a sample library, he says, not real pianos), a celesta, sometimes a harp and an electric piano, playing precisely written arpeggios and scales, or patterns chosen at random from one chord.
    *Works on:* texture, rhythm, colour.
    *Why:* to bring out the speed, the fast activity of the brain. [LAist] *(his words)*
    *How:* layer several keyboard instruments, each running its own quick arpeggio or scale on the same chord, some written exactly, some free within the chord.
    *Where:* *The Imitation Game*. [LAist]
16. **A small orchestra behind.** The orchestra behind the keyboards is not too big.
    *Works on:* colour, loudness.
    *Why:* so that the music does not become pompous or overwhelming. [LAist] *(his words)*
    *How:* keep the orchestra modest under a busy foreground.
    *Where:* *The Imitation Game*. [LAist]
17. **The composer as a character.** He tries to be a character in the film, not a composer throwing music at it; with Wes Anderson, he says, they lay tools and toys on the ground and build a strange castle.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: how he places his music in a story.
    *Where:* his account of his work. [LAist]

## Numbers

- **The Shape of Water:** twelve flutes, no other woodwinds. [MovieMusicUK; RTÉ]

## What he does not do

- **"Period music":** he calls it a trap. [Far Out]
- **The same instruments from one film to the next.** [Tatler]

## Too well known to borrow

No theme of his is called famous by the sources read. His two Oscar-winning scores are *The Grand Budapest Hotel* and *The Shape of Water* (Wikipedia); their themes, like everything of his, are in copyright and are not to be taken.
