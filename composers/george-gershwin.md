## Techniques

### His songs at the piano (*George Gershwin's Song-Book*, 1932)

1. **Variations on his own songs, made at the piano for company.** He played his songs often at private parties and so came to make many variations on them; the *Song-Book* prints eighteen of them twice over, the simple sheet-music version and the arrangement as he played it, the only printed form of his improvising.
   *Works on:* form, texture.
   *Why:* printed sheet music is kept simple for buyers with "little hands"; his playing at parties brought "the desire for complication and variety", and printing the variations might prolong the songs' life. [Predota, quoting the preface] *(his words)*
   *How:* take a song's tune and make a piano piece of it as one would play it for friends: the tune kept, and around it more and more complication and variety.
   *Where:* the *Song-Book*. [Predota]
2. **The refrain alone, in a major key, the tune on top.** The arrangements leave out the songs' verses and keep the refrain; all are in major keys; the tune is in the top line of the right hand, easy to follow. The tempo marks are in English.
   *Works on:* form, melody.
   *How:* use only the refrain; set it in a major key; keep the tune as the highest notes of the right hand.
   *Where:* the *Song-Book*. [Predota]
3. **A striding left hand under a syncopated right.** The left hand strides in broken chords while the right hand syncopates, with appoggiaturas adding excitement; elsewhere a walking bass in parallel sixths and tenths; boogie-woogie dotted sixteenths passed between the hands; an arpeggiated left hand that adds a playful, almost comic touch.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture.
   *How:* in the left hand, a low note on the beat and a chord on the off-beat; or a bass that walks in sixths or tenths; in the right hand, the tune pushed off the beat, with leaning notes before the main ones.
   *Where:* the *Song-Book*, in general (the article names no bars). [Predota]
4. **Blue thirds and sevenths.** Many flattened thirds and sevenths.
   *Works on:* harmony, melody.
   *How:* in a major key, let the third and the seventh be flattened now and then, in the tune and in the chords.
   *Where:* the *Song-Book*. [Predota]
5. **Learned from the ragtime pianists:** some of the effects of his transcriptions come from the players before him; he names Mike Bernard, Les Copeland, Melville Ellis, Lucky Roberts, Zez Confrey, and Arden and Ohman, and says that the popular piano style began with ragtime.
   *Works on:* background.
   *How:* not a technique: where the manner comes from.
   *Where:* the preface. [Predota, quoting the preface]

### The Three Preludes (1926)

6. **A blues lullaby.** The second Prelude, in C sharp minor, has a subdued tune winding over a smooth, steady bass line. Its harmonies and its tune are built on thirds, stressing the seventh and the blues scale's doubt between major and minor. A middle section changes key, speed and material; the opening comes back shorter, otherwise unchanged, and the piece ends with a slow climb up the keyboard. He called it "a sort of blues lullaby".
   *Works on:* melody, harmony, form.
   *How:* a soft tune over a bass that moves evenly, the chords made of thirds up to the seventh, the third of the key wavering between major and minor; a contrasting middle; the opening again, shorter; a slow rising close.
   *Where:* Prelude no. 2. [Wikipedia, Three Preludes; Predota, Three Preludes]
7. **One five-note blues motif for a whole piece,** with syncopations from the Brazilian baião and chords with flattened sevenths; in three parts, but heard as a fantasia, because snippets of virtuoso playing (repeated notes, octaves, scales, crossed hands) each last only a moment.
   *Works on:* melody, rhythm, form.
   *How:* build all the tune of a quick piece from one short blues figure; give each passage its own brief display and move on.
   *Where:* Prelude no. 1, in B flat. [Wikipedia, Three Preludes]
8. **A question in the minor, an answer in the major,** which fight when they return in octaves; the major wins.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *How:* pair a phrase harmonised in the minor with an answer in the major of the same keynote; bring both back loud, and let the major end it.
   *Where:* Prelude no. 3, in E flat minor (he called it "Spanish"). [Wikipedia, Three Preludes]

### In general

9. **What his music fuses,** as one writer puts it: the flattened notes and syncopations of the blues and of ragtime, the "aching cadences" of Hebrew chant, the harmonies of Chopin, Liszt and Debussy, and the patter of Gilbert and Sullivan.
   *Works on:* background.
   *How:* not a technique: a list of sources.
   *Where:* his music in general. [Wikipedia, George Gershwin, quoting Rodney Greenberg]

## Walks through whole works

**Prelude no. 2**, as the article walks it: the tune over the steady bass; a middle section in another key and speed, with other material; the opening again, shorter; a slow ascent of the keyboard to close. [Wikipedia, Three Preludes]

## Numbers

- The three Preludes together last about five minutes. [Wikipedia, Three Preludes]
- The *Song-Book*: eighteen songs, roughly in the order they were written. [Predota]

## What he does not do

*In the Song-Book.*

- Print a song's verse: only the refrain.
- Set a song in the minor.

## Too well known to borrow

- ***Rhapsody in Blue***, his most popular work; ***An American in Paris***; ***Porgy and Bess***; and the songs, many of which became standards. [Wikipedia, George Gershwin]
