## Techniques

1. **Counterpoint in the tango, sometimes a strict fugue.** His counterpoint, often complex and virtuosic, sometimes follows the rules of a fugue; more often it lets each player of the group be heard as a voice of its own. He brought fugato sections and classical forms into the tango.
   *Works on:* texture, form.
   *Why:* the counterpoint lets each performer in the group assert his voice. [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write the tango as lines, one a player, that answer and cross one another; now and then let them enter one after another with the same subject, as in a fugue.
   *Where:* his mature works in general (the source names no bars); *Fuga y misterio*. [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla; Holten-Andersen; Baton Music]
2. **A fugue whose entries climb by fourths.** *Fuga y misterio* opens with its subject alone, in E minor; it is answered in A minor, then enters in D minor and in G minor, each a fourth above the last, the instruments piling up; then twice more in the home key, E minor, the sixth followed by a short coda. Six entries in all.
   *Works on:* form, harmony.
   *How:* state the subject alone; bring each new entry a fourth higher than the one before (E, A, D, G), the earlier voices going on; after the fourth entry, bring it back twice in the home key and close.
   *Where:* *Fuga y misterio*, the *Fuga*, in the arrangement for twelve cellos that the note describes. [Baton Music]
3. **The subject is agitated and jitters around the scale,** a solo that the other instruments then echo in turn. In the arrangement described it is played with a percussive bow scratch.
   *Works on:* melody, colour.
   *How:* write the subject in quick notes that run up and down the scale with small turns and repeated notes, nervous rather than smooth; give it first to one instrument alone.
   *Where:* *Fuga y misterio*, the opening. [Baton Music]
4. **After the fugue, a slow song that vanishes.** The *Misterio* is based on a slow, lyrical melody, related to the main theme of the opera's heroine, that gradually vaporizes; it begins as a solo over a ghostly sound (harmonics, in the arrangement), with a fiery transition, full of pulsing accents, before the last entry.
   *Works on:* form, melody.
   *How:* follow the fast fugue with a slow, singing tune for one instrument over very little, and let it thin out to nothing.
   *Where:* *Fuga y misterio*, the *Misterio*. [Baton Music]
5. **3+3+2 against even eighths.** In tango the eight eighths of a bar are grouped 3+3+2 (the milonga's rhythm), set against groupings of 4+4 or a steady 2+2+2+2. In *Otoño porteño* the piano and double bass hold the 3+3+2 (bars 21 to 28) while another layer keeps the even 2+2+2+2 of the introduction.
   *Works on:* rhythm.
   *How:* in a bar of eight eighths, accent the first, fourth and seventh in the piano and the bass; let another part keep accents on every second eighth at the same time.
   *Where:* *Otoño porteño*, the end of section A (bars 21 to 28). [Holten-Andersen]
6. **The piano as a drum, the bass as weight.** The piano tends to serve throughout as a percussive rhythmic backbone; the double bass parts are usually of little interest in themselves but give the ensemble a rugged thickness. In *Otoño porteño* the bass marks beats 2 and 4 with percussive sounds.
   *Works on:* rhythm, texture, colour.
   *Why:* the bass's percussive accents hold up the 2/4 metre under the displaced rhythms. [Holten-Andersen] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* give the piano short, accented chords in the tango's rhythm, not a tune; give the double bass plain, low notes with percussive accents on the second and fourth beats.
   *Where:* his quintet works in general [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla]; *Otoño porteño*, the introduction and the coda [Holten-Andersen].
7. **A violin that plays rhythm, not tune.** In *Otoño porteño* the violin's first part is a percussive line rather than a melodic one, and in the coda (bars 100 to 107) it plays two rhythmic units of two bars each that are percussion only.
   *Works on:* rhythm, colour.
   *How:* give the violin, for a passage, short struck notes on one or two pitches in a rhythm of its own, two bars long and repeated, against the others' tango rhythm.
   *Where:* *Otoño porteño*, the introduction and bars 100 to 107. [Holten-Andersen]
8. **A circling bass,** the passacaglia's way of a bass line that comes round again under a sequence of chords, prevails in most of his mature works. In *Otoño porteño*'s section C an ostinato in the bass drives the music through its modulations, which rise every four bars, the tension growing to the climax.
   *Works on:* harmony, form.
   *Why:* the rise to the climax is held up by the modulations every four bars; the bass's ostinato is what carries the music through them. [Holten-Andersen] *(an analysis)*
   *How:* write a short bass figure that repeats; move the whole passage up a step in key every four bars over it, louder each time, until the climax.
   *Where:* his mature works in general [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla]; *Otoño porteño*, section C [Holten-Andersen].
9. **The slow section stands still.** In *Otoño porteño*'s slow section B nothing is displaced: the layers agree, and with no regular figure in the bass the music comes to an even more stationary point, a moment of uneasy rest before the storm.
   *Works on:* rhythm, form.
   *Why:* without displaced layers or a regular unit in the bass, nothing pushes the music on. [Holten-Andersen] *(an analysis; a listener)*
   *How:* in the slow part, take away the cross-rhythms and the bass's pattern; let all the parts move together, or hold.
   *Where:* *Otoño porteño*, sections B and B'. [Holten-Andersen]
10. **Fast parts rough, slow parts sung.** The fast sections stress gritty tango rhythms and harsh, angular melodic figures; the slow ones usually give the tune to the string instrument of the group. He scores the tango's rubato rather than leaving it to the players.
    *Works on:* melody, rhythm.
    *How:* in quick music, angular tunes with leaps and hard accents; in slow music, a singing line for the violin; write the pushing and pulling of the beat into the note values.
    *Where:* his works in general (the source names no bars) [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla]; the scored rubato [Holten-Andersen, citing Kutnowski].
11. **The close without the dominant.** In place of the traditional tango's cadence, the dominant to the tonic, he moves from a diminished chord on the keynote to the tonic chord without its third.
    *Works on:* harmony.
    *How:* at the last cadence, go from a diminished chord built on the keynote to an open fifth on the keynote, without the dominant chord and without the third.
    *Where:* *Otoño porteño*, the close. [Holten-Andersen]
12. **Extended harmonies and dissonance,** modal harmonies and scales; in some works twelve-tone rows and free improvisation.
    *Works on:* harmony.
    *How:* use chords with sevenths, ninths and added notes freely, and modes as well as major and minor.
    *Where:* his works in general (the source names no bars). [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla]
13. **The quintet:** bandoneon, violin, piano, electric guitar and double bass, his preferred group, which most critics take as the most successful for his works: a sound close to chamber music, without a singer, with jazz-like improvisation. *María de Buenos Aires* used a larger group, with flute, percussion, more strings and three voices.
    *Works on:* colour, background.
    *How:* for his manner, the five instruments of the quintet; the bandoneon as the lead.
    *Where:* the quintet years; *María de Buenos Aires*. [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla; Holten-Andersen]
14. **Where it came from:** Bach from his childhood (he was taught to play Bach on his bandoneon); counterpoint from Nadia Boulanger, who encouraged him to stay with the tango, recognising that his talent lay there; jazz; the milonga.
    *Works on:* background.
    *How:* not a technique: the sources of his counterpoint and his rhythm.
    *Where:* [Wikipedia, Astor Piazzolla; Baton Music]

## Walks through whole works

***Fuga y misterio*** (from *María de Buenos Aires*), as the programme note describes the twelve-cello arrangement: the subject alone in E minor; answered in A minor; entries in D minor and G minor (all twelve playing by the fourth); a fifth entry in E minor; the *Misterio*, a slow solo over harmonics; a fiery transition; the sixth entry, in E minor; a short coda. [Baton Music]

***Otoño porteño***, as Holten-Andersen analyses it: an introduction with percussive violin and bass; section A, the 3+3+2 in piano and bass against even eighths (bars 21 to 28), a modulation to D minor prepared at bar 33 and a return home by B7 at bar 37; section B, slow, consonant and still; section C, like a development, the bass ostinato driving modulations every four bars to the climax; B'; a coda in which the violin plays two percussive two-bar units (bars 100 to 107). [Holten-Andersen]

## What he does not do

*In the works the sources describe.*

- End with the traditional tango's cadence, the dominant to the tonic (*Otoño porteño*).
- Give the double bass a tune.
