  The fourth is a process of adding notes into silence, which Reich's *Drumming* (1971) already does, in his words "the process of gradually substituting beats for rests (or rests for beats)", the pattern built up "from a single drum beat" (his note at boosey.com). The first three are the ones taken further below.

## Candidates

### D. The tune made of the holes

- **The idea, from 1:** a dance repeated, with notes left out of it at each repetition; the notes left out, taken in order, are themselves a second tune. The listener hears a dance with holes, then, at the end, the holes sung: the second tune played whole by another instrument, or the dance whole at last with that tune now heard inside it.
- **The nearest found:**
  - Kubik's "inherent rhythms" in East and Central African xylophone music: rhythms the listener hears that no player plays, made by the interlocking of what is played. Kubik, on hearing a recording of his own playing: rhythms "which I was sure that none of us had played, while on the other hand the rhythms which we had actually played were inaudible on the tape" (*African Music* 3.1, from the search excerpt only). A tune made of what is played, not of what is left out.
  - Reich's "resulting patterns": "sub-melodies heard within repeated melodic patterns" ("Music as a Gradual Process", from the excerpt quoted in a course reading). The same, inside a repeated pattern.
  - Reich's "reduction" in *Drumming*: "rests are gradually substituted for the beats, one at a time, until only a single beat remains" (his note at boosey.com). Holes made in a repeating pattern one at a time, as here; but the holes make nothing of their own. This is the nearest: what is new in D, if anything, is only that the holes spell a tune.
  - The hocket: one line divided between voices (on the pages: Monk 12, "a self-hocket, then a hocket between singers").
  - Suppressed notes: a note a melody avoids, saved for its peak (the Beatles' pentatonic tunes, *Popular Music*, from the abstract only); pattern completion in Bartók and Stravinsky, a missing note whose arrival is a cadence (Wikipedia, "Pattern completion"; an analysis of Stravinsky's *Symphonies of Wind Instruments*, from the search excerpt only).
- **Not found** in three searches (a melody formed by the notes omitted from a repeated pattern; inherent and resulting patterns, and Reich's substitution of rests for beats, for the nearest).
- **What could go wrong:** a hole is heard as a hole, not as a note; whether a listener can gather holes into a tune before the reveal is not known, and the reveal may be all that is heard (a trick once, not a piece). 44's whole piece of holes was 3.5: "It was ok." In the sketch Philip heard the holes in a quick dance built on repetition; a slow one would show less.

### E. The bell that is retuned until it rings the tune

- **The idea, from 2 and 3:** one bell (a struck chord, always the same strokes) comes back through the piece; each time it is retuned a little, a note at a time and by less than a half step, until its stroke is the tune's opening. Or the other way round, from 34: the bell stays, and the line ends each phrase by sliding into the note the bell has left ringing, the bell setting where each phrase lands.
- **The nearest found:**
  - Scordatura: a string instrument tuned differently for a piece or a movement (Biber, Bach, Vivaldi's concerto op. 9 no. 6 with its G string tuned up to A, Mahler's fourth symphony; Wikipedia, "Scordatura"). A tuning fixed for the piece, not one that moves through it.
  - Tenney's *Diapason*: a band of a low note's harmonics that "moves very gradually from one pitch position to another within the harmonic series", the strings retuned in scordatura (a programme note, from the search excerpt only).
  - Piece 67 itself: one bell, one note retuned, by a half step.
  - Slides on the pages: Wolfe's "slides between notes", Weinberg's lines "that slide and wail"; none slides into another instrument's ringing note.
- **Not found** in one search (a bell or gong retuned step by step during a piece until it matches the melody). Weaker than D: one search, and the second form (a line that lands on a drone's note) is close to every music sung over a drone: the sliding into a drone's pitch is common in Indian classical singing, which was not searched here.
- **What could go wrong:** in the renderer a bell's pitch is the nearest recorded note resampled, so a bell retuned by a quarter of a tone is possible, but it may sound out of tune rather than retuned; the change has to be followed across returns a minute apart.
